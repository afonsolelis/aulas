2. Learning objective

By the end of the meeting, students should be able to apply the concepts of Agile Project Management: Fundamentals and Practical Applications to the project, within the scope of Scrum, Kanban and agile project management practices applied to software development. The deck states the goal as applying agile frameworks to plan, execute and improve iterative software delivery in team projects.

Observable outcomes

  • Explain the problems of the waterfall model that motivated the Agile Manifesto and state its four values and twelve principles.
  • Describe the roles, artifacts and events of Scrum and the lifecycle of a sprint from planning to retrospective.
  • Write a user story in the format of user, goal and value, with acceptance criteria in Given, When and Then, and check it against the INVEST criteria.
  • Estimate stories with story points, Planning Poker or T-shirt sizing.
  • Organise a Kanban board with WIP limits and relate WIP, throughput and lead time through Little's Law.
  • Distinguish velocity, lead time, cycle time and throughput and read a burndown chart and a cumulative flow diagram.
  • Write a Definition of Done for the team and recognise the anti-patterns of agile practice in the project.

3. Meeting outputs

User story of the project

One story of the group backlog rewritten in the canonical format, with acceptance criteria and an estimate.

Definition of Done

The agreement of the group on what done means, written from the example of slide 12 and section 5 of the material.

Agile checklist

The checklist of slide 14 and section 10 of the material marked by each group, separating practices already in place from those still missing.

Continuity

As the last meeting of the module, the lesson closes with the slide that gathers testing, data structures, operating systems, security and agile practice, and asks the groups to apply them together in the distributed systems project.

6. Facilitation script

  1. Present the agenda of slide 2 and contrast on slide 3 the sequential phases of the waterfall model with iterative cycles, using the Standish Group figure on features rarely or never used.
  2. Read the four values of slide 4, stressing the sentence of the manifesto that keeps value in the items on the right, and ask which of the twelve principles of slide 5 the groups already practise.
  3. Present the roles, artifacts and events of Scrum on slide 6 and the sprint lifecycle on slide 7, asking how long the sprints of the project are and whether each one produces a usable increment.
  4. Show the Kanban board of slide 9 and use Little's Law from section 4 of the material to explain why reducing WIP reduces lead time.
  5. Write a user story with the class from the template of slide 8, check it against INVEST and estimate it with Planning Poker.
  6. Present the metrics of slide 10 and ask which one the group could track with the data its board already holds, then present the tools of slide 11 with the recommendation chosen for the class.
  7. Read the Definition of Done of slide 12 and ask the groups what a story of their project must satisfy before it is considered done.
  8. Go through the anti-patterns of slide 13 and ask each group to recognise, without naming individuals, the one closest to its own practice.
  9. Ask each group to mark the checklist of slide 14 for its project, then close with the takeaways of slide 15 and the end of the module.

7. Methodology

The meeting follows the strategy recorded for this encounter, a dialogued exposition alternated with guided application to the group project and closed with a check of understanding. The exposition follows the historical order of the deck, in which agile practice appears as a response to concrete problems of plan-driven projects, so that each value, role or metric is presented together with the problem it addresses.

The second hour applies the content to the board and the backlog that the groups already maintain. Rewriting a real story, drafting the Definition of Done and marking the checklist make visible which practices the group already follows and which ones it adopts in name only, which is the distinction that the anti-patterns slide asks the class to recognise.

The sources record no graded activity for this meeting. Verification is formative and relies on the user story rewritten by each group, on its Definition of Done and on the agile checklist marked for its project.

10. Risks and contingencies

Exposition exceeding the deck agenda

Slides 10 and 11 can be summarised with a pointer to sections 6 and 7 of the material, preserving the time of application to the project.

Group without a maintained board

The group builds the board during the application block from the To Do, In Progress and Done columns of slide 9, with a WIP limit on the middle column.

Discussion of anti-patterns turning personal

The instructor keeps the discussion on the practice of the group and on the fix listed in section 8 of the material.

11. Closing

The meeting closes with the five takeaways of slide 15 and with the end of Module 5. Agile practice values people, collaboration and adaptability, Scrum gives rhythm through sprints, roles and events, Kanban reduces cycle time by limiting WIP, metrics such as velocity, lead time and throughput guide decisions, and team autonomy, real retrospectives and a Definition of Done distinguish agile practice from its anti-patterns.
