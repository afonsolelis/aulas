2. Learning objective

By the end of the meeting, students should be able to apply the concepts of Security in Web and Cloud Systems to the project, within the scope of cyber security based on CyBOK: OWASP Top 10, authentication, authorization, cryptography, cloud security and DevSecOps. The deck states the goal as recognising critical security risks and applying CyBOK-informed protections for web applications and cloud infrastructure.

Observable outcomes

  • Situate the topics of the lesson in the five categories and 21 knowledge areas of CyBOK version 1.1.
  • Recognise the ten risks of the OWASP Top 10 and explain how parameterized queries prevent SQL injection.
  • Distinguish stored, reflected and DOM XSS from CSRF and indicate the controls against each.
  • Distinguish authentication from authorization, describe the structure of a JWT and the role of OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect, and justify server-side permission checks.
  • Choose between symmetric encryption, asymmetric encryption and hashing for data in transit, data at rest and passwords.
  • Separate the responsibilities of the customer and of the provider in the cloud and recognise the common configuration mistakes.
  • Place SAST, SCA, SBOM, DAST, WAF and SIEM in the stages of a DevSecOps pipeline and configure CSP and HSTS headers.
  • Explain how a Backend for Frontend keeps secrets on the server and supports server-side rendering for indexing.

6. Facilitation script

  1. Present the agenda of slide 2 with the time of each block and the goal of the lesson.
  2. Introduce CyBOK on slide 3 by analogy with SWEBOK, and on slide 4 point to the knowledge areas that the lesson will cover.
  3. Go through the OWASP Top 10 on slide 5 and ask each group which risk it considers most likely in its own application, retaining A01, broken access control, as the risk that requires server-side permission checks.
  4. Show on slide 6 the input that bypasses the vulnerable login and the parameterized version that separates SQL structure from user data.
  5. Contrast XSS and CSRF on slide 7 as two forms of abuse of the trust between browser and server, with their respective controls.
  6. Use the table of slide 8 to separate the question of who the user is from the question of what the user can do, and present on slide 9 the JWT flow, its three parts and the rule against storing tokens in localStorage.
  7. On slide 10, ask the class which mechanism protects each kind of data: TLS for traffic, AES-256 for storage and bcrypt or Argon2 for passwords, which are hashed and never encrypted.
  8. Present the shared responsibility model of slide 11 and ask which of the common mistakes listed could occur in the project.
  9. Place the controls of slide 12 in the pipeline stages, show the headers of slide 13 and present the BFF of slide 14 as the layer that keeps API keys out of the browser.
  10. Ask each group to mark the checklist of slide 15 for its project and to name one missing control, then close with the takeaways of slide 16.

7. Methodology

The meeting follows the strategy recorded for this encounter, a dialogued exposition alternated with guided application to the group project and closed with a check of understanding. CyBOK provides the organising vocabulary: every topic of the deck is labelled with the knowledge area it belongs to, so that the class sees the OWASP risks, authentication, cryptography and cloud configuration as parts of a single body of knowledge.

Each risk is presented together with an example of attack and the corresponding mitigation, which allows the class to judge the mitigation by the mechanism it blocks. The checklist at the end converts the exposition into an inventory of the group project, in which each control is marked as implemented or missing.

11. Closing

The meeting closes with the five takeaways of slide 16: CyBOK organises security in five categories and 21 knowledge areas, the OWASP Top 10 relates risks to fixes, authentication and authorization are both mandatory and checked on the server, data is encrypted in transit and at rest while passwords are hashed, and the cloud combines shared responsibility with DevSecOps from design to deployment.
