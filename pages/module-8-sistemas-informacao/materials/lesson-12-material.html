Part IFoundations

1. How to read this material

This material accompanies Lesson 12, which has two hours. In the first, the professor prices on AWS the DataApp built in Lesson 7, tcg-dataapp, in four steps: the measurement of its workload, the description of five candidate architectures, their estimate in the AWS Pricing Calculator and in code, and the optimisation of the cheapest one. In the second hour each group repeats the four steps over its own DataApp.

Part I explains how a cloud bill is formed and where its prices come from. Parts II to V follow the four steps of the session, and Part VI gathers the activity of the groups, the prompts, the common mistakes and the references. Every price was read from the public AWS Price List on 08/11/2026 and every measure of the workload was taken on the same day; both change, and the AWS Pricing Calculator remains the reference for a real estimate. The lab of the session holds the same model in a page, where every quantity and every price can be edited.

2. A bill is a sum of quantities times unit prices

AWS does not sell a server for a fixed monthly fee. Each service counts one or more billing dimensions, such as the hours in which an instance runs, the gigabyte-seconds of memory used by a function or the gigabytes kept in a bucket during a month, and the bill multiplies each counted quantity by its unit price. The total of a month is the sum of these products, and estimating it means first estimating the quantities that the workload will produce, because the unit prices are public and fixed for a given region and pricing model.

The consequence for the design of a DataApp is that two architectures that run the same code can differ in cost by orders of magnitude, depending on which dimensions they turn into cost. A dimension counted by time, such as instance hours, is paid whether the DataApp works or not; a dimension counted by use, such as GB-seconds of a function, is paid only while it works. 3. Pricing models: on-demand, commitments and Spot

The unit price of compute depends on how the capacity is bought. On-demand capacity has no commitment and, for Linux instances, is billed per second with a minimum of one minute. A commitment lowers the rate in exchange for a promise to pay it for one or three years, every hour, whether the capacity is used or not: a Reserved Instance or an EC2 Instance Savings Plan is tied to an instance family in a region, while a Compute Savings Plan is a commitment in USD per hour that applies to EC2, Fargate and Lambda in any region, with a smaller discount. Spot capacity is spare capacity sold below on-demand, which AWS can reclaim with two minutes of notice.

Modelt4g.small, us-east-1, per hourCondition
On-demand$0.0168no commitment
Compute Savings Plan, 1 year, no upfront$0.0121commitment in USD per hour; EC2, Fargate and Lambda
Reserved Instance, standard, 1 year, no upfront$0.0105tied to the instance type and region
EC2 Instance Savings Plan, 1 year, no upfront$0.0105tied to the instance family and region
Spotabout $0.0072average saving of 57% by the Spot Instance Advisor; interruptible

A commitment is worth its discount only above a utilisation of break-even, which is the ratio between the committed rate and the on-demand rate: for the reserved t4g.small, 0.0105 / 0.0168 = 62.5%. An instance that runs fewer than 62.5% of the hours of the year costs less on-demand. The Price List also carries free allowances, which are the zero-price first tiers of some services: one million Lambda requests and 400,000 GB-seconds per month, 14 million invocations of EventBridge Scheduler and 100 GB of data transfer out per month, aggregated across the account. Because these allowances belong to the account and are shared by all its workloads, an estimate for one DataApp is shown both with and without them.

4. Where the prices come from

AWS publishes its prices in two forms. The AWS Pricing Calculator, at calculator.aws, is a form in which the user chooses a service, a region and a configuration and receives a monthly and an annual total; an estimate can be saved with a share link and exported as CSV, PDF or JSON, and it is the format in which an estimate reaches a manager or a partner. The AWS Price List is the same catalogue as files: one JSON or CSV document per service and region, available at pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com without an account or a key, in which each price has an offer code, a usage type, a unit and the date on which the offer was published.

The lab of this session uses a snapshot of the Price List taken by capture/snapshot_prices.py, kept in data/prices.json with 17 KB. Most offers are small, but the EC2 offer of one region has about 480 MB and the Savings Plans offer about 440 MB, so the script reads the EC2 file as CSV in a stream, keeps only six instance types and deletes the Savings Plans files after reading them. Every price of the snapshot keeps its provenance, as in the entry below, and the lab shows it next to the value.

Part IIThe workload

5. Measuring the workload

The first step measures the quantities of the workload before any price is looked up, since every line of the bill multiplies one of them. Some quantities are measured by running the DataApp, and others are decided by the group or assumed, such as the number of runs per month or the volume read by the users; the document of the workload records the origin of each, so that a reader of the estimate can tell a measure from a hypothesis. 6. What the measures imply

Three readings of the table guide the rest of the session. Thirty runs of forty seconds add up to twenty minutes in a month of 730 hours, so the DataApp works during 0.05% of the month; any resource billed by time is paid almost entirely for idle hours. The CPU time of a run is less than a third of its wall time, because most of the run waits for the downloads of tcgcsv, so a larger machine would shorten the run very little. And the peak memory of 392 MB, not the average, sets the smallest size that can run the DataApp: with a margin of 30% it needs about 510 MB, which fits an instance or a function of 1 GB.

The 21.6 MB downloaded per run cost nothing, because AWS does not charge data transfer into its regions. They still matter, because they explain the duration of the run, and a DataApp that downloaded a hundred times more data would take longer and pay more in every architecture billed by duration.

Part IIIThe architectures

7. Five architectures for the same package

The second step describes architectures that run the same package and differ in what they keep billed. Architecture A keeps an EC2 instance on, with its volume and public address, and runs the package by cron, as the README of Lesson 7 suggests. B uses the same instance but starts it for each run and stops it afterwards. C runs the package as a Lambda function, started by EventBridge Scheduler, which reads the DuckDB file from S3 at the start of the run and writes it back with the marts at the end. D runs the package in a container as an ECS Fargate task, started by the same scheduler, with the image kept in ECR. E adds to D a database of RDS for PostgreSQL, which serves the gold layer to readers that need SQL.

8. Limits that decide the choice

Price is not the only criterion, because each service imposes limits on the workload. A Lambda function stops any invocation at 15 minutes and has between 128 MB and 10,240 MB of memory, with an ephemeral disk in /tmp of 512 MB by default; the forty seconds and 392 MB of tcg-dataapp fit with a large margin, but a DataApp whose run approaches fifteen minutes needs another service. A Fargate task has no limit of duration and includes 20 GB of ephemeral storage in its price, but bills from the start of the image pull to the end of the task, with a minimum of one minute, so a short run pays for the start of its container. An EC2 instance has no limit either, and its boot adds to the billed time of architecture B.

Some resources are billed while idle without being visible in the diagram. An EBS volume is billed while its instance is stopped; a public IPv4 address is billed by the hour while it is attached; a database instance is billed every hour; and a NAT gateway or a load balancer, which these architectures avoid, are billed every hour as well. The estimate must include them, because in a DataApp that works 0.05% of the month they can be most of the bill.

Part IVThe estimate

9. The estimate in the AWS Pricing Calculator

The calculator is filled by hand, one service at a time, and the procedure below produces an estimate that can be compared with the code and shared with the partner.

  1. Open calculator.aws and choose Create estimate.
  2. Add a group per architecture, for example B · EC2 per run, so that the totals of the architectures stay apart.
  3. For each service of the architecture, choose Add service, select the region (US East, N. Virginia, or South America, São Paulo) and fill the configuration with the quantities of the workload: for EC2, the instance type, the usage in hours per month and the volume; for Lambda, the architecture, the number of requests, the duration and the memory; for Fargate, the operating system, the CPU architecture, the number of tasks per month, the duration, the vCPU and the memory; for S3, the storage and the requests.
  4. Save the estimate, copy the share link and export it as CSV to estimates/<architecture>-<region>.csv.
  5. Repeat for the other region.

The calculator does not know the workload, so it gives back exactly the quantities that it received. Its value lies in the unit prices and in the rules of each service that it applies, such as the free tiers and the minimum durations; the risk lies in a quantity typed wrong or a resource forgotten, which is why the estimate is checked against the model in code.

10. The cost model in code

The model of the lab, cost_model.py, turns each architecture into a list of lines, each one a quantity, a unit and a unit price taken from data/prices.json, and sums them. Writing the estimate in code makes it repeatable: a new measure of the workload or a new snapshot of the prices produces a new estimate in a second, and a test can check that a total equals the sum of its lines computed by hand. Part VOptimisation

12. Right-sizing

Right-sizing chooses the smallest resource that meets the measured demand with a margin. For tcg-dataapp the peak of 392 MB with 30% of margin needs about 510 MB, which a t4g.micro of 1 GiB holds; the t4g.small of 2 GiB first chosen doubles the memory without shortening the run, since the run waits for the network. In architecture A the change lowers the total from $16.55 to $10.42 per month, a saving of 37%. In C the same reasoning keeps the function at 1 GB; less memory would also give the function a smaller share of CPU, which Lambda allocates in proportion to the memory.

13. Scheduling and idle resources

The largest saving of the session comes from not paying for idle hours. Starting the instance for each run, architecture B, reduces the bill of A from $16.55 to $0.66 per month, 96% less, because the instance and its address are then billed for about ten hours a year instead of 8,760. What remains in B is the volume of 8 GB, billed while the instance is stopped. The public IPv4 deserves its own line in every estimate: since February 2024 AWS charges $0.005 per hour for each public address in use, which in A is $3.65 of a total of $16.55.

14. Commitments and the utilisation of break-even

A commitment lowers the rate of a resource that is used most of the time. In A, a reserved t4g.small for one year without upfront lowers the total from $16.55 to $11.96, and in E a reserved db.t4g.micro lowers it from $14.03 to $10.81. The decision depends on the utilisation of break-even, the ratio of the committed rate to the on-demand rate: 62.5% for the t4g.small and 72.5% for the db.t4g.micro (0.0116 / 0.016). A resource that runs fewer hours costs less on-demand, so a commitment for architecture B, whose instance runs 0.1% of the hours, would multiply its cost instead of lowering it.

15. Spot and idempotent loads

Spot lowers the price of an EC2 instance by the saving that the Spot Instance Advisor reports, 57% on average for t4g.small in us-east-1 on the day of the snapshot; Spot prices change, and the model treats this saving as an estimate. The cost of Spot is the possibility of losing the instance in the middle of a run. For tcg-dataapp the loss is acceptable, because the load of Lesson 7 replaces each batch inside a transaction and a rerun of the pipeline repeats only what failed, so an interrupted run can be retried without duplicating data. A DataApp whose load is not idempotent cannot use Spot safely.

16. Region and data residency

The same resources cost more in São Paulo than in Virginia: 1.48 times for A, 1.89 times for B and 2.09 times for E, while C and D cost a few cents in both. A region is chosen for more than price, however. The latency of the readers, the services available in the region and the contracts with the partner may require São Paulo. The LGPD does not forbid storing personal data outside Brazil, but it allows the international transfer of personal data only in the cases of its article 33, and a partner may require in contract that the data stay in the country. The ratio between the regions is the price of that requirement, and the estimate states it so that the partner can decide.

17. Storage classes and data transfer

A storage class with a lower price per gigabyte is not always cheaper. S3 Standard-Infrequent Access costs $0.0125 per GB-month in us-east-1 against $0.023 of Standard, but bills each object as at least 128 KB and at least 30 days, and charges each gigabyte read. The marts of tcg-dataapp are files smaller than 128 KB that are rewritten and read every day, so Standard-IA would cost more than Standard for them. Data transfer out to the internet costs $0.09 per GB in us-east-1 and $0.15 in São Paulo after the first 100 GB per month of the account; for a report read by a few people it is zero, but a DataApp that serves files to many readers must estimate it.

18. Sensitivity and the recommendation

An estimate is only as good as the quantities it assumes, and the ranking of the architectures changes with them. With 720 runs per month, one per hour, and 8,640, one every five minutes, the always-on architectures keep their cost while the architectures billed per use grow with the runs.

21. Common mistakes in an estimate

  • Pricing the machine and forgetting what it keeps billed while idle: volumes, public addresses, databases, NAT gateways and load balancers.
  • Sizing by the average memory instead of the peak, which fails the run, or by habit, which pays for unused capacity.
  • Comparing monthly totals of different workloads, for example one architecture with the free allowances and another without them.
  • Buying a commitment for a resource that runs fewer hours than its utilisation of break-even.
  • Choosing Standard-IA for small files that are read every day.
  • Typing a price from memory or from an old page, without the source and the date.
  • Presenting a single number without the condition under which it stops being valid.
