2. What the LGPD protects

The Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados Pessoais (Law 13,709/2018) regulates every operation on personal data carried out in Brazil, about people in Brazil or for the offer of goods and services to them, whether the operation is collection, storage, combination, analysis or publication. A DataApp that receives the customers of a partner is therefore processing personal data from its first load, and the team that builds it answers for how it does so. The law rests on three definitions of its article 5, which decide which rules apply to each column of a table.

DefinitionArticleConsequence for the DataApp
personal data: information about an identified or identifiable natural personart. 5, Ithe whole law applies; an internal id that links rows to a person is personal data
sensitive personal data: racial or ethnic origin, religious belief, political opinion, union membership, health, sex life, genetic or biometric dataart. 5, II; art. 11stricter legal bases, usually specific and highlighted consent
anonymised data: data about a person who cannot be identified by reasonable technical means available at the timeart. 5, III; art. 12outside the law, as long as the anonymisation cannot be reversed with reasonable effort
pseudonymisation: the data loses the possibility of association with a person except by additional information kept separatelyart. 13, §4still personal data; reduces the risk, does not remove the duties

The distinction between the last two rows matters most for a DataApp. A table in which the customer became a key that the company can still link back to the person is pseudonymised and remains under the law; only an aggregate from which no reasonable means recovers a person leaves it. Step 04 builds both, and the dashboard of the shop receives only the second.

3. Principles, legal bases and accountability

Article 6 lists ten principles, and five of them translate directly into controls of a database. The principle of purpose requires a legitimate and explicit reason for each processing; necessity limits the data to the minimum that the purpose needs; security and prevention require technical measures against unauthorised access and incidents; and accountability requires the controller to demonstrate that the measures exist and work. A team that keeps a column without a purpose violates the second principle, and a team that cannot show who accessed a record fails the last one.

Each processing also needs one of the legal bases of article 7, or of article 11 for sensitive data. The card shop uses four: the performance of the contract of sale for most of the customer data, a legal obligation for the CPF on the invoice, consent for marketing, and the specific consent of article 11 for the accessibility need of a customer, which is health data. A legal basis is attached to a purpose, so the same column may be lawful for the store and unlawful for the analysts.

4. Data governance: jobs, duties and roles

Data governance assigns to each person a job with duties over the data: who owns a dataset, who keeps it correct, who operates the platform, who consumes it and who answers to the authority. These duties stay on paper unless the database enforces them, and it does so only when each job is a role with its own privileges. The lab therefore creates one role per job and makes each person a member of a job, so that replacing a manager is a change of membership and not a review of every grant.

5. Security in layers

Information security is usually described by three properties: confidentiality, by which only authorised people read the data; integrity, by which only authorised changes happen; and availability, by which the data is there when needed. This lesson concentrates on the first two. A single control rarely guarantees them, because every control fails sometimes, through a careless grant, a bug or a stolen password. Defence in depth stacks independent controls, so that the failure of one is contained by the next.

The six steps of the lab form such a stack. The privilege of the table decides whether a role reaches it; the policy of the row decides which part of it the role sees; the masking of the columns decides what an authorised analyst learns about a person; the audit log records what happened; and the access test detects when a change has weakened any of the layers. Step 06 shows the stack at work: a careless grant opens the first layer, the second still returns no row, and the test fails anyway because the policy demands that both hold.

7. Step 01: the inventory of personal data

No access can be designed before the team knows what it keeps. The first control is a table of the database, gov.data_catalog, with one row per column of the schema shop: its category under article 5, whether it is a direct identifier, a quasi-identifier or neither, its purpose, its legal basis, its retention and what the analytics layer does with it. Two queries test the inventory against the catalogue of PostgreSQL, so that a column added later without classification fails the test.

A direct identifier points at one person alone, as the CPF or the e-mail do. A quasi-identifier does not, but a few of them together do: among 60 customers, the birth date, the city and the store narrow the search to one or two people. Latanya Sweeney showed in 2000 that the ZIP code, the birth date and the sex identify most of the population of the United States, and the same arithmetic applies to any small population. The inventory marks the quasi-identifiers so that step 04 generalises them.

8. Step 02: roles and least privilege

The principle of least privilege gives each job only the privileges its purpose needs. PostgreSQL implements it with roles: a role without LOGIN represents a job, a person is a role that is a member of it, and every privilege is granted to the job. The lab creates ten roles, moves the tables of shop from the superuser to app_owner, revokes from PUBLIC every privilege on the schemas and tables involved, and grants to each job what the table of governance allows.

Three details of PostgreSQL decide whether the grants mean what they seem to mean. A role needs USAGE on a schema before any table of it can be read, so a SELECT on a table of a schema without usage grants nothing. Every new function is executable by PUBLIC unless that privilege is revoked, which steps 03 and 05 do for each function they create. And ALTER DEFAULT PRIVILEGES decides the privileges of objects that do not exist yet: every view that analytics_owner creates in analytics is readable by the analysts without a grant that someone could forget.

The ETL is the clearest case. It inserts customers and orders and reads only the stores it validates against; it cannot select a customer, not even one it inserted. If its password leaks, no customer is exposed, which is the purpose of least privilege.

9. Step 03: row level security

A grant decides which tables a role reaches; a policy decides which rows of them it sees. With row level security enabled on a table, a row is visible to a role only when some policy for that role returns true for the row, and a table with RLS and no policy for a role shows it nothing. The superuser and the roles with BYPASSRLS ignore the policies, and the owner of the table ignores them too unless the table is set to FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY. The lab enables and forces RLS on customers and orders, so that even app_owner, which owns them, sees no customer.

The policy of the managers compares the store of the row with the stores of the acting role, read by the function gov.my_stores() from gov.store_staff, a table that the managers cannot read. The function is SECURITY DEFINER, so it runs with the privileges of its owner, and its search_path is fixed, so that nobody can make it call a function of the same name in another schema. Inside such a function current_user is the owner and not the caller, which is why gov.actor() reads the acting role from current_setting('role') and, with a real login and no SET ROLE, from session_user.

-- Lesson 13 · Module 8 · Information Systems · Inteli
--
-- GRANT decides which tables a role reads; a policy decides which rows. With RLS on,
-- a row is visible only when some policy of the role returns true for it, so a
-- table with RLS and no policy shows nothing to anybody but the superuser and the
-- roles with BYPASSRLS. FORCE extends the rule to the owner of the table.

-- Who works in which store. Kept in gov, which the managers cannot read.
CREATE TABLE gov.store_staff (role_name text NOT NULL, store_id text NOT NULL REFERENCES shop.stores, PRIMARY KEY (role_name, store_id));
INSERT INTO gov.store_staff VALUES ('manager_sp', 'sp'), ('manager_rj', 'rj'), ('manager_bh', 'bh');

-- The role that is acting. Inside a SECURITY DEFINER function current_user is the
-- owner of the function, so the role set by SET ROLE (or the login) is read instead.
CREATE FUNCTION gov.actor() RETURNS text
LANGUAGE sql STABLE
AS $$ SELECT coalesce(nullif(current_setting('role'), 'none'), session_user::text) $$;
GRANT EXECUTE ON FUNCTION gov.actor() TO PUBLIC;

-- The stores of the acting role. SECURITY DEFINER: the policy can read gov.store_staff
-- without granting it to the managers; search_path is fixed so nobody can hijack it.
CREATE FUNCTION gov.my_stores() RETURNS SETOF text
LANGUAGE sql STABLE SECURITY DEFINER SET search_path = pg_catalog, gov
AS $$ SELECT store_id FROM gov.store_staff WHERE role_name = gov.actor() $$;
REVOKE EXECUTE ON FUNCTION gov.my_stores() FROM PUBLIC;
GRANT EXECUTE ON FUNCTION gov.my_stores() TO store_manager;

10. Step 04: pseudonymisation, generalisation and aggregation

The analysts of the shop need to know how customers behave, not who they are. The analytics layer applies the decisions of the inventory with three techniques. Pseudonymisation replaces the customer id by a key, the first 16 hexadecimal characters of the SHA-256 of a secret pepper followed by the id. Without the pepper the key would be useless as protection, because there are only 60 ids and computing their 60 hashes reverses every key; with it, only the holder of the pepper can link a key back to a customer, which is exactly the "additional information kept separately" of article 13, §4.

Generalisation reduces the precision of the quasi-identifiers: the birth date becomes an age band (18-24, 25-34, 35-49, 50+), the moment of an order becomes its day in Unix time (ordered_unix / 86400 * 86400), and the e-mail is masked as its first letter and its domain. Aggregation with a minimum group size, the k-anonymity of Samarati and Sweeney, publishes only the groups with at least k distinct customers: with k = 5, of the 11 segments of store and age band, 7 are published and 4 are suppressed, with 7 customers in them.

The three views belong to analytics_owner, which reads shop and has BYPASSRLS. A view runs with the privileges of its owner, so the analysts read the views without any grant on the raw tables, and the dashboard reads only the aggregate. The pepper is a table that only analytics_owner reads; in production it belongs to a secret manager or to an environment variable outside the repository, since whoever holds the pepper and the keys can reverse every pseudonym. In the lab the pepper is drawn again at each replay, so the keys change after a reload, while the customers behind them do not.

11. Step 05: the audit log

Article 37 asks for a record of the processing operations, and the accountability principle asks the controller to be able to show it. The log of the lab, audit.events, records who acted, what was done, on which table and row, when (Unix time), with what purpose and on which columns. Two mechanisms feed it. A trigger on shop.customers records every INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE; a read, which no trigger sees, can only be recorded by the code that serves it, so the contact data of a customer is served only by shop.lookup_customer(id, purpose), which checks that the actor manages the store of the customer, demands a purpose of at least ten characters and writes a READ event.

The log records the names of the changed columns and never their values. A log with the old and the new e-mail would be a second store of personal data, with its own retention and its own leaks, and would have to be protected as carefully as the table it audits. The log is also append-only: a trigger refuses every UPDATE and DELETE of audit.events, the owner included, and only the DPO reads it.

-- Step 05 · Audit log: who did what, when and why
-- Lesson 13 · Module 8 · Information Systems · Inteli
--
-- LGPD art. 37: the controller keeps a record of the processing operations; art. 46:
-- technical measures protect the data from unauthorised access. Two kinds of event:
--   changes, caught by a trigger on every INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE of shop.customers;
--   reads of contact data, which a trigger cannot see, so they happen only through a
--   function that checks the store, demands a purpose and writes the event.
-- The log records the names of the changed columns and never their values, otherwise
-- the log itself would become a second copy of the personal data.

CREATE TABLE audit.events (
  event_id   bigserial PRIMARY KEY,
  at_unix    bigint NOT NULL DEFAULT extract(epoch FROM now())::bigint,
  actor      text   NOT NULL,
  action     text   NOT NULL,
  table_name text   NOT NULL,
  row_key    text,
  purpose    text,
  columns    text[]
);
REVOKE ALL ON audit.events FROM PUBLIC;
GRANT SELECT ON audit.events TO dpo;

-- Append-only: an UPDATE or DELETE of the log fails for every role, the owner included.
CREATE FUNCTION audit.refuse_change() RETURNS trigger
LANGUAGE plpgsql AS $$ BEGIN RAISE EXCEPTION 'audit.events is append-only'; END $$;
CREATE TRIGGER append_only BEFORE UPDATE OR DELETE ON audit.events
  FOR EACH STATEMENT EXECUTE FUNCTION audit.refuse_change();

-- Changes. SECURITY DEFINER: the trigger writes the log, which the actor cannot touch.
CREATE FUNCTION audit.log_change() RETURNS trigger
LANGUAGE plpgsql SECURITY DEFINER SET search_path = pg_catalog, audit, gov
AS $$
DECLARE
  changed text[];
  rec jsonb := to_jsonb(coalesce(NEW, OLD));
BEGIN
  IF TG_OP = 'UPDATE' THEN
    SELECT array_agg(n.key ORDER BY n.key) INTO changed
    FROM jsonb_each(to_jsonb(NEW)) AS n
    JOIN jsonb_each(to_jsonb(OLD)) AS o USING (key)
    WHERE n.value IS DISTINCT FROM o.value;
  ELSE
    SELECT array_agg(k ORDER BY k) INTO changed FROM jsonb_object_keys(rec) AS k;
  END IF;
  INSERT INTO audit.events (actor, action, table_name, row_key, columns)
  VALUES (gov.actor(), TG_OP, TG_TABLE_SCHEMA || '.' || TG_TABLE_NAME, rec ->> 'customer_id', changed);
  RETURN NULL;
END $$;
CREATE TRIGGER audit_customers AFTER INSERT OR UPDATE OR DELETE ON shop.customers
  FOR EACH ROW EXECUTE FUNCTION audit.log_change();

12. Step 06: the policy as an automated test

A policy that nobody tests drifts with the next grant, because the person who adds a privilege on a busy day rarely reviews the whole policy. The last control writes the expected access of each role as data, in gov.expected_access, from the table of governance and before the first run, and a function runs every probe as every role and compares. The expectations must come from the policy; copying them from a first run would turn the test into a photograph of whatever the database does, errors included.

gov.try_as(role, sql) runs the statement twice, first as the superuser and then under SET LOCAL ROLE, each time inside a block of PL/pgSQL with an exception handler, which PostgreSQL executes as a subtransaction. The block always ends by raising a known exception, so the subtransaction is always rolled back: an INSERT or a DELETE that succeeds leaves no row, not even an event in the audit log. The outcome is denied when the statement fails, none when it returns no row, some when it returns fewer rows than the superuser and all otherwise.

--
-- A security policy that nobody tests drifts with the next GRANT. The policy of the

  ('read audit log',   8, 'SELECT * FROM audit.events'),

-- Runs a statement as a role and classifies the outcome against the superuser:
-- denied (error), none (0 rows), some (fewer rows than the superuser) or all.
CREATE FUNCTION gov.try_as(p_role text, p_sql text) RETURNS text
LANGUAGE plpgsql AS $$
DECLARE
  n bigint; base bigint; msg text;
BEGIN
  BEGIN
    EXECUTE p_sql; GET DIAGNOSTICS base = ROW_COUNT;
    RAISE EXCEPTION USING ERRCODE = 'P0001', MESSAGE = 'rollback';
  EXCEPTION WHEN raise_exception THEN NULL; WHEN others THEN base := NULL;
  END;
  BEGIN
    EXECUTE format('SET LOCAL ROLE %I', p_role);
    EXECUTE p_sql; GET DIAGNOSTICS n = ROW_COUNT;
    RAISE EXCEPTION USING ERRCODE = 'P0001', MESSAGE = 'rollback';
  EXCEPTION
    WHEN raise_exception THEN
      IF SQLERRM <> 'rollback' THEN RETURN 'denied'; END IF;
    WHEN others THEN
      RETURN 'denied';
  END;
  IF n = 0 THEN RETURN 'none'; END IF;
  IF base IS NULL OR n >= base THEN RETURN 'all'; END IF;
  RETURN 'some';
END $$;
REVOKE EXECUTE ON FUNCTION gov.try_as(text, text) FROM PUBLIC;

  FROM gov.expected_access AS e
  JOIN gov.probes AS p USING (probe)
  CROSS JOIN LATERAL (SELECT gov.try_as(e.role, p.sql) AS observed) AS o
$$;

18. Mistakes found in the rehearsal

The traps below appeared while the lab was rehearsed on 08/11/2026, and the prompts carry the corresponding adjustments.

A grant that grants nothing

The first version of the regression of step 06 granted SELECT on the customers to the analyst and expected a failure of the test, which did not come: without USAGE on the schema, the privilege on the table had no effect. The schema is a layer of its own, and the regression now grants both.

Who is the current user

An audit trigger written with current_user recorded postgres for every event, because a SECURITY DEFINER function runs as its owner. The acting role is read from current_setting('role'), with session_user as the fallback for a real login without SET ROLE.

The owner under FORCE

With FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY and no policy for app_owner, the owner of the tables cannot read nor insert a row. The expectation of the test for the owner's insert is therefore denied, not all, and a team that forces RLS needs a policy, or a separate role, for the maintenance it still has to do.

Superusers ignore every policy

The session of the lab is the superuser, which bypasses RLS even on a forced table. Every proof of a policy must run under SET ROLE, and the scripts end with RESET ROLE, so that the next step does not run as the last role used.

20. Checklist

  • Every column has a category, a purpose, a legal basis and a retention.
  • Every privilege belongs to a job, and nothing is left to PUBLIC.
  • The tables with personal data have RLS enabled and forced.
  • No policy depends on a setting that the user can change.
  • The analytics layer has no direct identifier, and its keys use a secret.
  • The dashboard reads only groups with at least five people.
  • Every change and every read of contact data is in an append-only log without values.
  • The expected access is a table, and a test compares it with the database after every change.
  • No secret is in the repository.
