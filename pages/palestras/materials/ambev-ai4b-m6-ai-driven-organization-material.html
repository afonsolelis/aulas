2. Why AI governance is a leadership matter

An AI initiative produces three effects that a traditional report does not. It moves data to new uses, often data that was collected for another purpose; it automates part of a decision, so that a score or a generated text shapes what a person decides or what the company does to a customer; and it exposes the company to new failure modes, some of them specific to generative AI. Each of these effects carries a risk, and a risk has to be accepted, reduced, transferred or refused by someone with the authority to do so.

This is the reason the programme places governance in the module on leadership. Governance, in the sense used here, is the set of decisions, roles and controls that define which AI uses an area accepts, under what conditions and with whose approval. The technical controls are designed by data, security and legal teams, but the decisions that those controls implement are business decisions: the purpose of the initiative, the level of error the area tolerates, the point at which a person reviews the output and the person who answers for the system after it is launched. When these decisions are not taken explicitly, they are taken implicitly by whoever configures the tool, which is usually the person with the least authority to take them.

The frameworks of this block serve the partner as sources of questions, so that he or she recognises when an initiative enters a regulated area and asks the right people before approving it.

3. LGPD: personal data in a commercial operation

The Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados Pessoais, Lei nº 13.709 of 14 August 2018, regulates the processing of personal data in Brazil. It defines personal data as information related to an identified or identifiable natural person (art. 5, I), and it therefore does not protect legal entities as such. A beverage company sells mostly to outlets registered as companies, which could suggest that the law has little to say about its commercial data. That conclusion does not hold, because behind each outlet there are natural persons whose data circulates in the same systems: the owner of a small bar who is a sole proprietor, the contact who places orders by phone, the delivery staff and the company's own sales representatives. Consumer data collected through apps, promotions or loyalty programmes is personal data in full and often reaches a large scale.

Data in a commercial operationPersonal data under the LGPD?Why it matters for AI
Volume, channel and tier of an outlet registered as a companyGenerally noCan be used in models with fewer restrictions, although it may be linked to personal data elsewhere.
Name, phone and tax number (CPF) of the owner of a small outletYesA model that profiles the outlet may be profiling a person.
Performance, route and visit records of sales representativesYesSupports decisions about employment, which the EU AI Act classifies as high risk.
Consumer data from apps, promotions and loyalty programmesYesLarge volume, consent and transparency obligations, and exposure in case of leakage.

The law organises processing around principles and legal bases. Article 6 lists ten principles, among them purpose, adequacy, necessity, transparency, security, prevention, non-discrimination and accountability. Article 7 lists ten legal bases that authorise processing, such as consent, the performance of a contract, compliance with a legal obligation and the legitimate interest of the controller, which article 10 restricts to concrete situations and to data strictly necessary for the purpose. For an AI initiative, the principle of purpose has the most direct consequence. Data collected to deliver products and invoice an outlet was collected for that purpose, and its later use to train a model or to profile the owner has to be compatible with the original purpose or rest on its own legal basis. The decision about the legal basis is taken with the data protection officer (the encarregado, art. 41), but the business owner of the initiative has to state the purpose clearly enough for that decision to be possible.

The law also requires the controller to keep records of its processing operations (art. 37) and allows the national authority to request a data protection impact report (art. 38), which describes the processing, the data used, the risks to the data subjects and the measures adopted. An initiative that will use personal data to train or feed a model benefits from preparing this report before the pilot, because the questions it asks are the same that the partner should ask in any case.

4. LGPD, AI outputs and the role of the ANPD

An AI output can itself be personal data, as in the case of a churn score attached to the name of an outlet owner, and it can be the basis of a decision about a person. Article 20 of the LGPD gives the data subject the right to request the review of decisions taken solely on the basis of automated processing of personal data that affect his or her interests, including decisions that define a personal, professional, consumer or credit profile. The controller must provide, when requested, clear information on the criteria and procedures used, subject to commercial and industrial secrets (art. 20, §1). For an area that plans to let a model decide which customers receive an offer, a credit limit or a visit, this article means that someone must be able to explain the criteria and that a route for review must exist before the first complaint arrives.

Two further obligations bear directly on generative AI. Article 46 requires security measures able to protect personal data from unauthorised access and from accidental or unlawful situations, and article 48 requires the controller to communicate to the national authority and to the data subjects any security incident that may cause relevant risk or damage to them. The ANPD regulated this communication in 2024 (Resolução CD/ANPD nº 15/2024). A spreadsheet of customer data pasted into a public chatbot, or an internal assistant that exposes documents to employees without access to them, can constitute such an incident.

The Autoridade Nacional de Proteção de Dados (ANPD) is the body that regulates, supervises and sanctions under the LGPD. It was created by Lei nº 13.853/2019 and became an autarchy of special nature with Lei nº 14.460/2022. Article 52 of the LGPD lists the sanctions it may apply, from a warning to a simple fine of up to 2% of the revenue of the company, group or conglomerate in Brazil in its last fiscal year, limited to R$ 50 million per infraction, and including the publicisation of the infraction and the suspension or prohibition of the processing activity. The ANPD has already acted on AI: in July 2024 it ordered, as a preventive measure, the suspension of Meta's use of personal data of Brazilian users to train generative AI models (Despacho Decisório nº 20/2024/PR/ANPD), and the Senate version of PL 2338/2023 assigns to it the coordination of the national system of AI regulation.

Question for the partner

If an outlet owner or an employee asked tomorrow why the system treated him or her in a certain way, who in the area would answer, and with what information?

5. AI regulation: the EU AI Act and Brazil's PL 2338/2023

The European AI Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, known as the AI Act, is the first comprehensive AI law of a major jurisdiction. It entered into force on 1 August 2024 and organises its obligations by the risk that the use of a system creates. Its territorial scope reaches providers that place AI systems on the European market and deployers located in the Union, and also providers and deployers established outside the Union when the output of their systems is used in it (art. 2). For a Brazilian company, the Act matters in two ways: directly, when its systems or their outputs are used in Europe, as happens in groups with European operations; and indirectly, because PL 2338/2023 follows the same risk-based logic, so the European text is the most developed reference for what Brazilian regulation may require.

TierWhat it coversExample close to a commercial operationConsequence
Unacceptable riskPractices prohibited by art. 5, such as manipulation that exploits vulnerabilities, social scoring and emotion recognition in the workplaceA system that infers the emotions of sales staff from video during visitsProhibited since 2 February 2025; fines up to EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide turnover (art. 99)
High riskSafety components of regulated products and the uses listed in Annex III, among them employment and worker management, and creditworthiness of natural personsA model that ranks sales representatives for promotion or dismissalRisk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, and conformity assessment
TransparencySystems that interact with people or generate synthetic content (art. 50)A chatbot that answers outlet owners; generated images in a campaignPeople must be told that they are interacting with AI, and synthetic content must be marked
Minimal riskAll other usesDemand forecasting by product and region; delivery route optimisationNo specific obligation beyond AI literacy; voluntary codes of conduct

General-purpose AI models, such as the large language models behind Copilot, have their own obligations for providers, which apply since 2 August 2025. Article 4 requires providers and deployers to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy of the staff who operate AI systems on their behalf, an obligation in force since 2 February 2025 and directly related to programmes such as this one. Most obligations for high-risk systems were scheduled to apply from 2 August 2026, and those for high-risk systems embedded in regulated products from 2 August 2027. In November 2025 the European Commission proposed, in a package of simplification measures, to adjust the timetable of the high-risk obligations, and the dates in force should therefore be checked on the official sources before they are written into a plan.

Brazil's PL 2338/2023

Brazil has no general AI law in force at the time of writing. The main proposal is Projeto de Lei nº 2338/2023, introduced in the Federal Senate in May 2023 and based on the work of a commission of jurists that studied the subject in 2022. The Senate approved a substitute text in plenary in December 2024 and sent it to the Chamber of Deputies, where it was under examination when this material was written. A bill can be amended, merged with others or left without a vote, so the partner should treat its content as an indication of direction and check the current status on the website of the Chamber of Deputies before quoting it.

The text approved by the Senate classifies systems by risk, prohibits uses considered of excessive risk and imposes stricter duties on high-risk uses, including an algorithmic impact assessment. It gives people affected by AI systems rights such as information, explanation and human review of decisions, and it creates a national system of AI regulation and governance coordinated by the ANPD, with the participation of sectoral regulators. Until a law is approved, the LGPD already applies to every AI use that processes personal data, and consumer, labour and competition law continue to apply to decisions supported by AI.

6. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework

Laws say what is required, and management frameworks say how an organisation can organise itself to meet those requirements and to manage risks that no law covers. The AI Risk Management Framework of the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST AI 100-1, January 2023) is voluntary, independent of sector and widely used as a reference. It describes the characteristics of a trustworthy AI system (valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed) and organises the management of risk into four functions.

Govern

Establishes the culture, the policies, the roles and the accountability for AI risk. It is a cross-cutting function, present in the other three. For the partner, it asks who is accountable for the system, which policy applies and how the area decides which risks it accepts.

Map

Establishes the context: what the system is for, who uses it, who is affected by its outputs, which data it uses and what happens when it is wrong. A large part of the risk is identified here, before any metric is calculated.

Measure

Produces evidence about the identified risks, with quantitative and qualitative methods, before launch and during use. It asks which metrics show that the system works and stays reliable, fair and secure, and who tests it.

Manage

Allocates resources to the risks according to their priority, decides which risks are treated, transferred or accepted, and defines the response to incidents and the conditions under which the system is withdrawn.

The functions connect with what the partners did on Day 2. The business case of the Caderno already asked for the risks of data, privacy and adoption (Map), for the indicator that shows whether the initiative worked (Measure) and for the condition under which the partner would stop it (Manage). What the framework adds is Govern: the explicit decision about who answers for the system and under which policy. In July 2024 NIST published a Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) that applies the same functions to generative systems and lists risks specific to them, among them confabulation, the term the document uses for the production of confidently stated but false content, data privacy and information security. Organisations that need a certifiable management system can use ISO/IEC 42001:2023, which specifies the requirements of an AI management system in the same format as other ISO management standards.

7. Typical risks of generative AI and what leadership owns

Generative AI tools reach the daily work of the areas faster than earlier analytical systems, because they require no project to be used: a person with access to Copilot can start using it on the same day. This speed is useful and also explains why four risks appear as soon as a team adopts these tools. The OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications, in its 2025 edition, ranks prompt injection first and sensitive information disclosure second, and lists misinformation among the ten.

RiskHow it happensControl that leadership can require
Prompt injectionInstructions hidden in an email, a document, a spreadsheet or a web page are read by the assistant or the agent as if they came from the user and change its behaviour. The risk grows when the assistant can act, for instance by sending messages or changing records.Agents receive only the permissions their task requires, and actions that send, pay, approve or delete require confirmation by a person.
Data leakageConfidential prices, contract terms or personal data are pasted into a tool outside the company environment, or an internal assistant exposes documents to people who should not see them because the underlying permissions were too broad.A data classification that says what may enter each tool, and a review of access permissions before an assistant is enabled over the company's documents.
HallucinationThe model produces a number, a source or a conclusion that sounds plausible and is false. Module 3 discussed the verification strategies that mitigate it.Every number and source in an output that supports a decision is checked against the data, and the person who checked it is named.
Shadow AITeams adopt tools that nobody approved, usually because the approved ones are not available, are slow to configure or do not cover the task.A list of approved tools that is easy to use, a fast route to request a new tool and a policy that people know. Prohibition without an alternative tends to move the use out of sight.

The division of work between the partner and the specialist teams follows from the previous sections. The partner decides the purpose of the initiative and the decision it supports, which risks the area accepts and under which conditions it stops, where a person reviews the output before it affects a customer, an outlet or an employee, and who in the area is accountable for the system after launch. The partner delegates, and later checks, the legal basis and the impact report to the data protection officer and the legal team, the access, logging and security controls to technology and security, the metrics and the monitoring of the model to the data team, and the training and communication to the managers of the area.

An example from the case

In the Maré Alta case, Daniel Okamoto proposes a retention offer of R$ 2,000 for every outlet that the churn model flags. The governance questions come before the discussion of value: who approved the use of the score in a commercial routine, since the case document states that the model has been running without being used in any routine; what data about the owners of the outlets the model uses, and on which legal basis; how an outlet that does not receive the offer, or receives it on the basis of a wrong score, could contest the decision; and who monitors the model once the offer is running. These questions do not decide whether the proposal is good, which was the subject of the morning. They show what would have to be in place before any version of it could be implemented.

8. Practice 1: risk review of the Day 2 initiative

The first practice brings the block back to the Caderno. Each partner returns to the initiative written on Day 2 and checks it against five questions drawn from the frameworks of this block, adding to the risk table of the Caderno what was missing. The work is done in pairs at the table, for fifteen minutes, and the last five minutes of the block are used to hear two or three partners say which question changed their view of the initiative. Partners who did not complete the Day 2 section use the decision they chose on Day 1.

  1. Does the initiative process personal data, of whom, and for what purpose? Is the purpose compatible with the purpose for which the data was collected? (LGPD, arts. 6 and 7)
  2. Does any output of the initiative decide something about a person or an outlet without human review, and how could that person contest it? (LGPD, art. 20; EU AI Act)
  3. In which tier of the EU AI Act would this use fall if it were deployed in Europe? (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)
  4. Which of the four generative AI risks applies, and which control would reduce it? (OWASP; NIST AI 600-1)
  5. Who in the area is accountable for the initiative after launch, and under which condition would it be stopped? (NIST AI RMF, Govern and Manage)

Answers that the partner does not know are marked with a question mark, and next to each one the partner writes the name or the function of the person who would know. This list becomes useful in the third block, when the partner writes what has to be unblocked.

9. Prioritising initiatives by impact and feasibility

A partner who leaves the programme usually has more initiatives than capacity, and choosing between them by the enthusiasm of whoever proposes them repeats the situation of the Maré Alta case, where three executives defended three uses of the same budget with readings of the data that the others disputed. The impact and feasibility matrix is a simple instrument to make the criteria of the choice explicit before the choice is made.

The matrix places each initiative on two axes. The impact axis estimates the value the initiative would create if it worked: money or time saved, the number of decisions it improves and how central those decisions are to the targets of the area. The feasibility axis estimates the probability that the initiative works with the resources available: whether the data exists and can be trusted, whether the tools are available in the company, the effort of people, the regulatory risk examined in the first block, and the probability that the team will adopt the result in its routine. The combination gives four quadrants, each with a different treatment.

QuadrantTreatment
High impact, high feasibilityStart now, with a pilot, an indicator agreed in advance and a stop condition.
High impact, low feasibilityPlan for a later horizon and start with what removes the barrier, which is often the data or the governance.
Low impact, high feasibilityDo it only if it is cheap and builds capability that larger initiatives will need.
Low impact, low feasibilitySet aside and record why, so that the discussion does not return every quarter.

Two cautions apply. The first is that the scores are estimates, and two partners scoring the same initiative will disagree. The disagreement is informative when the criteria are written down, because it shows which premise each person is making, in the same way that the Day 2 business case asked for the premise behind the expected gain. The second is that feasibility tends to be overestimated when it is reduced to technology. Module 4 discussed where data and AI projects go wrong, and the causes listed there, among them misaligned goals, data integration, privacy, ethics and the measurement of return, concern the organisation more than the model. The case offers an example: the case document records that the churn model of Maré Alta has been running since the beginning of 2026 without being used in any commercial routine, which is a problem of adoption before it is a problem of the model.

10. A data and AI roadmap in three horizons, and Practice 2

The matrix says which initiatives deserve attention, and the roadmap says in which order. The idea of horizons comes from the three-horizon model of Baghai, Coley and White, written to manage portfolios of growth businesses, in which the first horizon defends the current business, the second builds emerging businesses and the third creates options for the future. Applied to a data and AI portfolio, the model helps the partner avoid two opposite errors: filling the plan with quick initiatives that never change an important decision, and committing the area to an ambitious initiative whose conditions do not yet exist.

HorizonIndicative periodWhat it containsExamples
H1 · Use what exists0 to 6 monthsInitiatives of high impact and high feasibility, with approved tools and data already availableA Genie space over trusted tables of the area; a Copilot routine for a recurring report
H2 · Build the conditions6 to 18 monthsData quality, integration of sources, governance and skills that the larger initiatives need, and the pilots that test themReconciling two source systems; defining the owner and the quality rules of a critical table
H3 · Change the decision18 months or moreInitiatives of high impact that are not yet feasiblePredictive models embedded in commercial routines; agents that act on behalf of the team

The periods are indicative and depend on the area. What matters is the dependency between horizons: each H3 initiative should point to the H2 conditions it requires, and each H2 condition should be started, even if modestly, within H1. The 30-60-90 plan of the Caderno corresponds to the first part of H1 seen at the scale of what the partner can start personally. Davenport and Ronanki, examining corporate AI projects, observed that ambitious projects aimed at transforming the business were less likely to succeed than projects that improved existing processes, and recommended building a portfolio in which these smaller projects create the capabilities that the larger ones need, which is the same dependency the horizons make explicit.

11. Caderno do Sócio, Day 3: the 30-60-90 plan

The third block is the protected hour of the Caderno. The 30-60-90 format comes from the literature on leadership transitions, where Watkins proposed organising the first ninety days of a new role in short periods with explicit goals, so that early results build credibility and reveal what needs to change. In the Caderno the format is applied to the area: the partner writes what he or she will do in the first thirty days, up to sixty days and up to ninety days, with three milestones in each period. Each milestone has three columns, the milestone itself, who is responsible and how the partner will know it happened, and the plan closes with a table of what has to be unblocked, with whom and when the conversation will take place.

The Caderno states three rules for the plan, and the professor reads them before the writing starts. The plan is of the area and not of the company. It avoids milestones that depend on approval from others or on new budget. It contains what the partner can start personally. The reason for these rules is practical: a plan whose first milestone depends on a committee meeting tends to stop at that milestone, while a plan that starts with what the partner controls produces evidence that later helps to obtain approvals and budget. The Caderno also asks the partner to write little and be specific, since an indicator with a name and a number helps more than a paragraph of intention.

What makes a milestone usable

A milestone is usable when another person could check whether it happened, and the third column of the Caderno exists to apply that test. The examples below are generic; each partner writes milestones for the decisions listed on Day 1 and for the initiative written on Day 2.

12. Integrated closing: the thread of the three days and the commitments

The last block returns to the sequence that organised the programme. On Day 1 the class learned to move from a business question to the decision it serves and to the data it requires, and each partner listed the recurring decisions of the area. On Day 2 the class analysed the extract with the tools of the company, interpreted the results and set aside at least one conclusion that did not survive checking, while each partner described an initiative with its value, premise, indicator, risks and stop condition. On Day 3 the class turned the analysis into a recommendation told as a narrative, and each partner turned the initiative into a plan. The closing asks each partner to name which part of that path will survive the return to daily work: asking for the decision behind a request, asking how a number was checked, or asking for the stop condition of an initiative before approving it.

The Caderno closes with a section of commitments, filled in during ten minutes of this block. The partner writes three commitments in the first person and with a date, one thing he or she will stop doing and one person of the team whom he or she will involve. The Caderno observes that these commitments belong to the partner and that nobody will check them, so they need to be small enough to be kept. Naming the thing to stop doing frees the time that the plan requires, since a plan that only adds tasks competes with the existing agenda, and naming a person of the team keeps the plan alive when the author's attention moves elsewhere.

The closing round takes twenty-five minutes. Each partner says, in under one minute, one commitment with its date and the thing he or she will stop doing, and nobody comments on the commitments of others during the round. The professor closes with what the round showed about the class and with the close of the programme. There is no hand-off to a following module: the plan written this afternoon is the part of the programme that continues in each area.

Continuity with the previous modules

The plan of the programme asks each professor to know what was decided in the earlier modules. Before the afternoon, the professor should obtain from the morning professor the business question formulated by the class on Day 1 and the recommendation presented on Day 3, and from the professors of Modules 2 and 4 an indication of how far the class went in the Caderno. The opening of the module uses these two elements: two partners read the question and the recommendation, so that the examples of the afternoon start from what the class decided. If many partners arrive with the Day 2 section incomplete, Practice 1 and Practice 2 can start from the decision chosen on Day 1, and the professor should say so explicitly instead of letting those partners wait.

Protecting the time of the Caderno

The first two blocks are the ones that should absorb any delay. If the governance block runs late, Practice 1 can be shortened to ten minutes, and if the planning block runs late, the sharing at the tables can be reduced; the sixty minutes of the Caderno and the closing round should not be reduced. The plan of the programme notes that the blank page is what requires intervention. During the writing, the professor walks the room and approaches partners whose page is still empty after ten minutes, asking which decision of Day 1 they would most like to change and starting the first milestone from it.

Collecting without grading

The programme has no grade, panel or competition, and the partner does not hand in the Caderno. The professor asks aloud, at the end of each block, who wants to read what he or she wrote: a question from the risk review, a matrix whose position changed, one milestone with its evidence. In the closing round, the professor avoids praising or ranking the commitments, since the purpose of the round is to make each commitment public, which increases the chance that it is kept.

Regulatory content

The partners are senior and new to the subject, and the regulatory content should stay at the level of the questions that a partner asks. Questions about the application of the LGPD to a specific initiative of Ambev should be referred to the company's data protection officer and legal team. The status of PL 2338/2023 and the dates of the EU AI Act may have changed after this material was written, and the professor should check both on the official sources in the days before the class.

Use of tools

The prompts in this material are optional and written for Microsoft Copilot in Ambev's environment. Partners should not paste confidential figures or personal data of customers or employees into any tool during the class, and the Caderno can be completed entirely by hand.
