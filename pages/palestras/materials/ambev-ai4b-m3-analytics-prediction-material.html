2. Case recap and the curated release

The morning opens with the question that the class recorded at the end of Module 1. The professor reads it aloud and writes it on the board, together with the decision it serves and the data it requires, because every analysis of the morning has to be traceable to it. A question that has become too broad overnight can still be narrowed at this point, which costs less than discovering at 12:30 that the analysis answered something else.

Helena's constraints have not changed. The envelope for invoice discounts and trade spend in FY27 is R$ 22 million, the same as in FY26; the board expects a credible path back to a gross margin of 48%; and Helena wants one recommendation that the group can explain, instead of a fourth opinion alongside the three she already has. The three proposals, summarised in the case document, each rest on one number. Rafael Monteiro reports that outlets in the Cooler Programme grew about 20% in FY26 against about 6% for the others; Beatriz Lacerda estimates that bringing off-trade discounts back to the FY25 level returns R$ 3.3 million to the margin; and Daniel Okamoto reports that the churn model is 87% accurate and proposes a R$ 2,000 retention offer for every outlet it flags.

From Day 2 the group works with the curated release, which the data team prepared after the review of the raw extract on Day 1. Its release note lists five corrections: region labels standardised, the duplicated South sales of March 2026 removed, the Northeast volumes of January to March 2025 converted from litres to hectolitres, one mis-keyed order corrected and the spend labels of the legacy system mapped to the standard ones. It also lists three things left unchanged pending a business decision: outlets without channel or income index, mostly from the legacy Northeast system; negative rows in the sales table, which are returns and credit notes; and trade spend booked for outlets no longer in the master table. The release note is therefore part of the data, since any result that touches these rows has to declare how it treated them. The structure of the five tables is described in the data dictionary.

3. Statistical reasoning for decisions

The block deals with interpretation and leaves calculation to the analysts. Its goal is that the partner learns to distrust a number that reaches the desk for the right reasons, and to ask the question that would make the number trustworthy.

3.1 Variability

Every business series moves even when nothing has changed in the business, and a difference between two numbers is informative only when it is large compared with that ordinary movement. Three sources of movement matter for the case. The first is the season: at Maré Alta a summer month can sell 40% more than a winter month, which is why the company starts its fiscal year in October, and a comparison between a January and a July mostly measures the weather. Comparing full fiscal years, or the same months of two years, removes that effect. The second is the spread between units. Two groups of outlets can have different average growth while most outlets of one group sit inside the range of the other, so the average alone says little about how distinct the groups are. The third is the size of the group, because the growth of ten outlets can swing with a single large customer, whereas the growth of a thousand moves little. For these reasons the number of outlets behind each percentage is part of the result and should be reported with it.

3.2 Confidence interval

An estimate computed from a sample comes with a range of values that the data cannot rule out, which is what a confidence interval describes. Its practical reading for a decision maker is that the decision should hold across the whole interval, and not only at the central value. Consider an illustrative pilot, whose numbers do not come from the case, in which a new 1-litre returnable bottle is tested in 40 bars and the average volume increases by 6%, with a 95% interval from −2% to +14%. The data is compatible with no effect and also with a large one, because 40 bars with very different volumes do not narrow the range enough. The investment case for a national rollout would be positive in only part of that interval, so the defensible decision is to extend or enlarge the pilot. Two consequences follow for the partner. An interval is wider when the sample is small or the units differ a lot, and an interval that contains zero does not prove that there is no effect; it only says that the data cannot yet distinguish the effect from its absence (Spiegelhalter, 2019).

3.3 Correlation and causation

When a group that received an action performs better than a group that did not, the action is only one of several explanations, and each alternative points to a comparison that can rule it in or out. Selection is the first alternative: when people choose which units receive the action, they tend to choose those they already expect to respond, and the comparison then measures their judgement as much as the action. Confounding is the second, since a third factor, such as location, channel or the potential of the outlet, can drive both the decision to join the programme and the growth that followed, and comparing outlets that share that factor removes its influence. The third is a prior trend: a group that was already growing faster before the action will look better afterwards, so the period before the action shows whether the gap is new. The fourth is reverse direction, in which the outcome explains the action, as when outlets that sell more are the ones that request a cooler (Pearl and Mackenzie, 2018).

The strongest evidence of cause comes from designs in which the action is assigned by a rule that does not depend on the expected result, such as a random draw among eligible outlets or a staggered rollout in which some outlets receive the action later than others. Such designs are standard in the evaluation of programmes and of online experiments (Angrist and Pischke, 2014; Kohavi, Tang and Xu, 2020). They matter for the case because any expansion of a programme is also an opportunity to measure it, which makes the way new outlets would be chosen a legitimate question for the board.

3.4 Significance

A statistically significant result indicates that a difference of that size would be unlikely if there were no real effect. The p-value is the probability of observing a difference at least as large as the one found under the hypothesis of no effect; it is not the probability that the effect is real, and the 0.05 threshold is a convention adopted by the field (Wasserstein and Lazar, 2016). Three cautions follow. With large tables, such as the 100,000 rows of monthly sales, almost any difference becomes significant, so the decision depends on the size of the effect in hectolitres or reais and on the range around it. When a team examines many segments, some will look significant by chance alone, at a rate of about one in twenty at the 5% level, so the partner asks how many cuts were tried before the one presented. Finally, significance measures only chance, and a difference produced by selection or confounding can pass the test and still mislead.

3.5 Practice 1: a test plan for the 20% vs 6% comparison

The practice takes 25 minutes and is done before any tool is opened, so that the group cannot adjust the test to the result it sees. In groups of four or five, the participants follow four steps.

  1. List the conditions that would have to hold for Rafael's comparison to show the effect of the coolers, such as comparable groups, no gap before the programme and enough outlets in each group (8 minutes).
  2. For each condition, name the column and the period of the extract that would test it, for example tier, region, channel or the change from FY24 to FY25 (8 minutes).
  3. Write what each test would show if the coolers caused the growth, and what it would show if they did not (4 minutes).
  4. Two groups read their plan aloud, and the class keeps the tests it will run in Practice 2 (5 minutes).

4. AI and data tools in the company

Databricks, Genie and Microsoft Copilot read the same curated release. They differ in who writes the query and in how easily the partner can see what was calculated, and that difference determines how each answer is checked.

Databricks is the data platform on which the tables live. Each of the five CSV files of the curated release is loaded as a table in a schema such as mare_alta_curated, and the column descriptions of the data dictionary are registered with the tables, which improves the answers of the assistant built on top of them. Analysts query these tables in SQL, and the partner rarely needs to open them directly.

Genie is the natural-language assistant of Databricks AI/BI. A Genie space is configured by an analyst over a set of tables, with instructions and example questions, and the user asks questions in ordinary language. Genie translates each question into SQL, runs it and returns the result together with the generated query, which the user can open (Databricks documentation). The visible query is what makes Genie suitable for this morning, because it allows the group to verify the period, the filters and the columns that were used without writing SQL.

Copilot in Excel works on data formatted as an Excel table and proposes formulas, pivot tables, charts and highlights in response to a request in natural language (Microsoft Support). The curated workbook already has one table per sheet. The sales table, with about 100,000 rows, is better handled in Genie, whereas the smaller tables, pos and churn_scores, suit Copilot well. Asking the same question in both tools is itself a check, since two tools that read the data in different ways are unlikely to make the same mistake.

4.1 Anatomy of a data prompt

A data question receives a checkable answer when it states four elements, because every element left open is filled by the tool with an assumption that the user does not see. The context names the company, the release and the tables. The period states the fiscal year explicitly, since Maré Alta's year runs from October to September and a tool left to itself will usually read "2025" as January to December, which mixes two fiscal years and splits a summer season in half. The metric names the column, the unit and the calculation, for instance whether returns are included or whether margin excludes trade spend, as it does in the data dictionary. The grouping names the cut and asks for the number of outlets in each cell. A fifth element, the request to show the query or formula, turns the answer into something the group can audit.

4.2 A verification habit

An answer from Genie or Copilot becomes evidence for the group only after three checks, which take a few minutes and catch most errors of period, filter and definition. The first is to ask for the query or the formula and read it, checking the date filter, the conditions and the columns, since a missing filter or a calendar year appears in the text of the query even when the number looks plausible. The second is to reconcile a total that is already known. The case document gives net revenue per fiscal year, R$ 145.0 million in FY24, R$ 167.9 million in FY25 and R$ 194.9 million in FY26, and the group reproduces these values before trusting any finer cut; if the total does not match, the group stops and finds out why, because the cause, whether returns, the release or the fiscal year, affects the finer cuts as well. The third is to re-ask the question differently, in other words, in the other tool or from the opposite direction, since a result that changes when the wording changes is not yet a result.

6. Predictive analysis for decision makers

A predictive model is judged by the decision it supports. The partner needs to know what kind of model it is, what errors it makes and what each error costs, while the details of training remain with the data team.

6.1 Types of machine learning

The type of learning follows from the business question. Supervised learning uses past cases whose outcome is known: when the outcome is a category, such as whether an outlet will stop buying in the next 90 days, the model is a classifier, judged by the confusion matrix and the metrics derived from it; when the outcome is a quantity, such as the hectolitres an outlet will buy next quarter, the model is a regression, judged by the size of its error in the unit of the business. Time-series forecasting, used for monthly demand by region in production planning, predicts the future of a series from its past and is judged on months it did not see during training. Unsupervised learning has no outcome to predict; clustering, for example, groups outlets with similar buying patterns, and its value lies in whether the segments are stable and usable by the commercial team (Provost and Fawcett, 2013).

Daniel's churn model is a supervised classifier. It learned from outlets that did or did not stop buying and gives each outlet a score between 0 and 1. The analytics team converts the score into a yes or no at a threshold of 0.5, and that choice of threshold is a business decision as much as a technical one, because it sets how many outlets are flagged and therefore how many offers are paid.

6.2 The confusion matrix, with a generic example

A yes-or-no prediction has four possible outcomes, and the confusion matrix counts them. The example below is illustrative and does not come from the case. A distributor predicts which of its 1,000 bars on 30-day credit will fall more than 60 days behind next quarter. In reality 50 bars do. The model flags 100 bars, and 30 of them are among the 50.

Flagged by the model30 (true positive)70 (false positive)100
Not flagged20 (false negative)880 (true negative)900
Total509501,000

The two errors have different costs. A false positive is a good customer whose credit is cut, which costs sales and the relationship; a false negative is a default that the company did not anticipate, which costs the unpaid invoices. Because the costs differ, the choice of which error to tolerate belongs to the business, which makes it together with the data team and expresses it in the threshold.

6.3 Accuracy, precision and recall

Every metric that a data team reports is a ratio between cells of the matrix, and each answers a different business question. Accuracy, the share of all predictions that were right, is (30 + 880) / 1,000 = 91% in the example. Precision, the share of flagged cases that were real, is 30 / 100 = 30%, and it answers the question of how many of the bars the company acts on actually needed the action. Recall, the share of real cases that the model flagged, is 30 / 50 = 60%, and it answers the question of how many of the bars that fell behind the company caught in time. The base rate, the share of real cases in the population, is 50 / 1,000 = 5%.

The example shows why accuracy misleads when the event is rare. A trivial rule that flags no bar at all is right on the 950 bars that paid on time, so its accuracy is 95%, higher than the model's 91%, although it catches no default. Accuracy should therefore always be compared with the accuracy of this trivial rule, which equals one minus the base rate, and a model's value lies in the precision and recall it achieves on the minority class (Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015).

The metrics become a decision when they are combined with costs. If each flagged bar receives a credit review visit costing R$ 300, the action costs 100 × R$ 300 = R$ 30,000 and finds 30 real cases, which is R$ 1,000 per case found. Whether that is worth paying depends on the loss avoided per case, and a lower threshold would flag more bars, raise recall, usually lower precision and increase the cost of the action. The partner's question to the data team therefore concerns the threshold that best balances the cost of acting on false positives against the loss from missing real cases.

8. Critical evaluation of outputs

In analytics, hallucination most often takes the form of a plausible number produced by a definition that the answer does not mention, which is why verification focuses on definitions, rows and comparisons.

8.1 How AI data tools go wrong

Studies of language generation describe hallucination as output that is fluent but not supported by the source (Ji et al., 2023), and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework lists validity and reliability among the characteristics of trustworthy AI, to be checked in the context of use (NIST, 2023). In the case, five kinds of failure are the most likely. The tool may read the wrong period, using the calendar year instead of the October to September fiscal year, which the group detects by reading the date filter in the SQL. It may use the wrong definition, computing margin with trade spend or using gross revenue where net revenue was meant, which the prompt prevents by naming the column and the formula. It may drop rows silently, excluding returns or outlets without channel through a filter or a join, which the request for row and outlet counts per group exposes. It may introduce an element that the extract does not contain, such as a column, a segment or a market benchmark, which the question about the table and column of each figure exposes. Finally, it may wrap a number in a causal narrative, writing that the coolers drove growth on the basis of a difference between groups, and the group then asks which comparison supports the causal word.

8.2 Reliability criteria

A conclusion enters the recommendation only when it meets five criteria. Its provenance is declared, meaning the release, tables and columns are named. A known total was reconciled before the finer cut. The same answer came back when the question was re-asked in other words or in the other tool. The comparison was made between comparable groups, before and after the action. Each group behind a percentage has enough outlets for the percentage to be stable. A conclusion that meets the criteria only in part is recorded as qualified, with the limitation stated next to it, and a conclusion that fails a criterion it depends on is discarded.

8.3 The analysis log and Practice 4

The analysis log is the record that the group carries to Day 3. It has one row per conclusion and keeps the discarded conclusions next to the kept ones, because a discarded conclusion is also a result: it tells the board which reading of the data the group examined and why it does not support the budget, which is part of what Helena asked for when she said she wanted to understand why the group believes what it recommends.

10. Facilitation notes for the professor

These notes concern the conduct of the session. The answer key of the case is kept in the professor area of the materials page, accessible with the shared passphrase, and is deliberately absent from this public page.

  • Confirm access to Databricks, Genie and Copilot before 09:00, as the programme plan requires. The Genie space should be created over the curated schema, with the column descriptions of the data dictionary registered, and each group needs at least one laptop with Genie and one with the curated workbook open in Excel. If Genie is unavailable, the prompts can be run in Copilot on the smaller tables, and the professor can run Prompts B to E on the projector.
  • Write the class's question of Day 1 on the board in the recap and leave it visible during the morning. When a group drifts into an analysis that does not serve the question, ask which part of the question the analysis answers.
  • In Practice 1, insist that the plan is written before the data is seen. Groups that propose only one test can be asked how the outlets of the programme were chosen and what the two groups looked like in the year before it started.
  • In Practice 2, do not give results and do not confirm or deny a number. When a group reports a finding, ask which check supports it and what the count of outlets is in each cell. When two groups disagree, ask each to show its query and let them find the difference.
  • In Practice 3, the questions are answered by the groups from their own matrix. The useful intervention is to ask what a model that predicts that nobody leaves would score, and to let the group compute it.
  • The case is not a competition and there is no grade. Ask in the read-outs who wants to share, and treat the discarded conclusion as a result of equal standing to the kept ones.
  • Protect the 35 minutes of the last block. If the morning runs late, shorten the exposition of the predictive block rather than Practice 4, since Day 3 depends on the log.
  • Pass to Prof. José Romualdo, before 14:00, the class's question, the main discarded conclusions and any remark about the Caderno; and keep the photos of the logs for Module 5.
