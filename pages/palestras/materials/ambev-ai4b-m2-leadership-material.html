About this session

Module 2 — Leadership in the Age of Data and AI · 07/12/2026 · Prof. Kim

Learning objective

By the end of the session, participants should be able to place an AI solution in a category (embedded, vertical, agentic) and in a level of capability and autonomy, break a role of their team into tasks to judge where AI automates, augments or leaves the work unchanged, describe what a leader has to ask, decide and protect when AI enters the routine of the area, and record in the Caderno do Sócio the recurring decisions of their area and at least one decision in which data or AI would change the outcome.

Session strategy

Strategic afternoon of four hours in which the Maré Alta case does not advance and serves only as a shared example. Three blocks combine short exposition with practices in pairs and at the tables: mapping the AI uses already present in each area by category and level of autonomy, breaking a real role into tasks, and discussing what the team needs from its leader through a Lean lens. The last hour is protected in full for the Day 1 section of the Caderno do Sócio, written individually with English prompts and timers, followed by a pair-sharing round, two readings aloud and the hand-off to Day 2.

The afternoon of Day 1 moves the analytical lens of the morning from the Maré Alta case to the area that each partner leads. Its purpose is to give the partner a vocabulary for AI solutions, a way to judge what AI changes in the work of a team and a clear view of what remains a responsibility of the leader.

The programme follows a rule of conduct that organises the three days: in the technical mornings the case advances, and in the strategic afternoons the Caderno do Sócio advances. 2. Generative AI applied to business

The analytical models that companies have used for decades, such as demand forecasts or credit scores, take structured data and return a number or a class. Generative models work differently, since they receive an instruction written in natural language and produce text, tables, code or images. This difference moves AI from the analytics department into tasks that until recently were done by people in every area, such as writing a report, summarising a meeting or drafting the answer to a customer.

Three trends follow from this change and matter to a partner. The first is that the cost of producing a first draft falls sharply. A first version of a monthly commentary or of a query costs seconds, and in consequence the scarce resource in a team shifts from producing the draft to checking whether it is correct. A team that adopts generative AI without strengthening its checking routine produces more material of unknown quality.

The second trend is that language becomes the interface to data and systems. Tools such as Databricks AI/BI Genie let a business user ask a question of a governed data space in plain language, without waiting for an analyst to write the query. The barrier to asking falls, and for that reason the quality of the question starts to matter more: a question that does not state the period, the metric and the grouping receives an answer that looks precise and may answer something else. Module 3, tomorrow morning, works on this point with the case data.

The third trend is that systems go beyond answering and start to execute. Recent systems call tools, read and write files and complete tasks of several steps. When a system acts, the questions of who authorised each step and who is accountable for its result stop being technical and become management questions.

The three trends have a common consequence. The decision about where AI enters a process is a decision about quality, accountability and risk, and it cannot be delegated entirely to the technology team, because only the business knows what an error costs in each process.

3. Categories of AI and the five levels

Embedded, vertical and agentic solutions

The programme uses three working categories to describe how an AI solution reaches the business. They are not exclusive, and a single solution can belong to more than one of them, but they help a partner understand what is being proposed when a vendor or an internal team presents a solution.

An embedded solution places AI inside software that the team already uses every day. Microsoft Copilot in Excel, Outlook and Teams is the example closest to the participants. Adoption tends to be fast because the workflow does not change, and for the same reason the use can spread through the area without anyone having decided where it is appropriate and where it is not. The leader's attention, in this category, goes to the rules of use: what information may be given to the tool and who reviews what it produces.

A vertical solution is designed or configured for a specific industry or function, with its data and vocabulary. In a beverage distributor, examples would be route planning for deliveries or demand forecasting by outlet and by season. A vertical solution solves a narrower problem than a general assistant and depends heavily on the quality of the domain data, which links this category to the discussion of data governance in Module 1.

An agentic solution receives a goal and decides the sequence of steps needed to reach it, using tools along the way. An agent could, for example, collect the week's sell-out, compare it with the plan by region and draft the alert to each regional manager. Anthropic, in a public guide on the subject, distinguishes workflows, in which the steps are defined in advance by people, from agents, in which the model chooses the path within the limits it was given. The distinction matters to a leader because the second kind of system needs explicit limits on the tools it can use, on the actions that require approval and on the condition in which it stops.

The five levels of AI

In July 2024 Bloomberg reported that OpenAI had shared with its staff an internal scale of five levels to track progress towards more capable systems, and that the company considered itself, at that time, at the first level and close to the second. The scale is one company's view and not a technical standard, so it should be read as a vocabulary for comparing what a system does, and not as a measurement. Its five levels, as reported, are the following.

LevelNameWhat the system doesQuestion for the partner
1ChatbotsConverses in natural language and answers requestsWho checks the answer before it is used?
2ReasonersSolves problems that require several steps of reasoningCan the reasoning be read and contested?
3AgentsTakes actions on behalf of a user over a period of timeWhich actions need human approval?
4InnovatorsHelps to produce new ideas and inventionsWho decides which idea is tested?
5OrganizationsPerforms the work of an entire organisationWhat remains a human decision?

The right-hand column was added for this programme and is not part of the reported scale. It shows that each level of capability raises a different management question, and that the questions become harder as the levels rise.

Capability and autonomy

A published framework from Google DeepMind, by Morris and co-authors, separates two dimensions that the five-level scale mixes: what a system is capable of doing and how much autonomy it receives when it is deployed. The authors describe levels of autonomy that go from AI as a tool, in which the person does the task and uses AI for a sub-task, through AI as a consultant, as a collaborator and as an expert, to AI as an agent that acts on its own. Their point is that a highly capable system can be deployed with little autonomy, and that the choice of autonomy belongs to whoever deploys it.

For a partner, the separation has a direct use. The same model can be deployed as a consultant in a pricing decision, in which the person reads its suggestion and decides, and as an agent in the scheduling of deliveries, in which it acts and reports. The level of autonomy should follow the cost of an error in each process, and the business is better placed than the vendor to judge that cost.

4. Practice 1: AI already present in your area

The first practice, in pairs and with twelve minutes, asks each partner to list two or three AI uses that already exist or are being discussed in the area, including informal ones such as a manager who drafts emails with Copilot. For each use, the partner names the category (embedded, vertical or agentic), the level of autonomy in the sense of Morris and co-authors, who checks the output today and what happens when the output is wrong. 5. Human + AI collaboration: tasks and skills

Labour economists study the effect of technology on work at the level of tasks rather than of occupations. The approach, formalised by Autor, Levy and Murnane in 2003 to explain the effect of computers on employment, treats a job as a bundle of tasks with different exposure to automation. Technology rarely replaces a whole occupation at once, and it usually changes the mix of tasks inside it, so that the same job title describes different work a few years later.

Eloundou and co-authors applied the same approach to large language models and published their analysis in Science in 2024. They estimated the exposure of each task of a detailed list of occupations to these models and concluded that exposure reaches a wide range of occupations, including those with higher wages and more education, which earlier waves of automation had affected less. Their measure concerns exposure, while actual replacement depends on cost, regulation and the decisions of each company.

The distinction between tasks and skills helps to read what happens inside a role. A trade marketing analyst in a beverage distributor consolidates spend by outlet, checks it against contracts, writes the monthly report and discusses the results with the sales managers. These are tasks, and each has a different exposure to generative AI. The same analyst knows which outlet deserves an exception, negotiates with a supermarket buyer and notices when a number is implausible. These are skills, which cross several tasks. When AI takes over a task, the skills used in it do not disappear; they move to the tasks that remain, such as checking the output, explaining it to the client and deciding the exception. The leader has to plan that movement, because otherwise the time freed by AI is absorbed by more of the same work.

Evidence from field experiments

Two field studies give a sense of how uneven the effect can be. Dell'Acqua and co-authors ran an experiment with consultants of the Boston Consulting Group who had access to GPT-4. On tasks inside what the authors call the jagged technological frontier, that is, tasks the model performs well, consultants with AI produced work of higher quality and finished faster. On a task chosen to fall outside that frontier, those who used AI reached the correct answer less often than those who worked without it, because many accepted the model's output without checking it. The frontier is described as jagged because tasks of apparently similar difficulty fall on different sides of it, and its position is not visible in advance.

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond studied the introduction of an AI assistant for customer support agents in a software company and found that it raised the number of issues resolved per hour, with the largest gains among less experienced agents. The authors interpret the result as a sign that the assistant spread practices of the best agents to the rest of the team. Read together, the two studies suggest that the same tool can help one task and harm another within the same role, so the decision about where to use it has to be made task by task and revisited as the tools change.

6. Career impact: displaced, augmented and builder

The programme uses three categories to describe what happens to a role when AI enters it. They describe trajectories of roles and say nothing about the merit of the people who hold them, and in most areas the three coexist.

Displaced

The role consists largely of tasks that AI performs at lower cost, such as keying orders or consolidating standard reports. The leader's responsibility is to anticipate the change and plan the transition of the people in the role, including the tasks they could take on.

Augmented

The core of the role remains, and AI takes over parts of it, such as the first draft of a promotion analysis. Productivity rises when the person learns to verify the output and uses the freed time for judgement and relationship.

Builder

The person configures assistants, agents and data spaces for colleagues, often without writing code, on platforms such as Microsoft Copilot Studio or a Genie space. The role depends more on knowledge of the business than on technical training.

The literature on management treats automation and augmentation as two choices about the same task rather than as two fixed types of technology. Raisch and Krakowski argue that organisations which focus only on automation lose the learning that comes from people working with the systems, and that those which focus only on augmentation may fail to capture efficiency where it is available; they call this tension the automation-augmentation paradox. Daugherty and Wilson, writing from consulting practice, describe the work in which people train, explain and supervise AI systems, and that description corresponds closely to the builder trajectory. Both sources lead to the same practical point: whether a role becomes displaced or augmented depends partly on how the leader designs the work around the tool.

8. The role of leadership in a data- and AI-driven organisation

Studies of organisations that compete on analytics and of companies that scaled AI converge on the finding that the obstacles which most often decide the outcome lie in management practices, such as the ownership of decisions and the redesign of work. Davenport and Harris, in their study of companies that compete on analytics, attributed a central role to senior leaders who demand evidence in decisions and sustain the investment over time. Fountaine, McCarthy and Saleh, writing in the Harvard Business Review from consulting work with companies that adopted AI, found that those which scaled it reorganised work across functions and treated the tool as one part of a change in the process. Four responsibilities of the leader follow from this literature and from the earlier blocks of the afternoon.

Make the decision explicit

A data or AI initiative should start from a recurring decision with a known owner and a known frequency. Without such a decision, a team produces dashboards and models that nobody uses. The Maré Alta case contains an example: the data team built a churn model that has been running since the beginning of 2026 without being used in any commercial routine, which means that nobody was assigned the decision the model was meant to inform. The Caderno do Sócio starts from the same principle, since its first section asks for the recurring decisions of the area before any mention of tools.

Ask before funding

The leader does not need to build an analysis, but needs to ask the questions that determine how far it can be trusted: which comparison supports a claim, which metric was used to judge a model and which premise an estimate depends on. In the Maré Alta case, each executive brings a claim based on the same data, and the chief financial officer has to decide which to fund. A leader in her position would ask, before committing the budget, how the outlets of the Cooler Programme were chosen and how the 400 new ones would be chosen, what the estimate of returned margin assumes about volume after the discounts change, and what accuracy a model that predicts that nobody leaves would reach. These questions are left open here on purpose, since the class examines the data on the morning of Day 2.

Redesign the work

Practice 2 showed that AI changes a role task by task. The leader decides what the freed time is used for, which tasks move between people and who becomes responsible for checking the outputs of AI. If no decision is taken, the change still happens, but it is shaped by individual habits rather than by the needs of the area.

Keep accountability with people

An output produced by AI should enter a decision only after a person accountable for it has checked it, and the leader defines who that person is in each process. This responsibility becomes heavier as autonomy increases, which is why the discussion of levels and autonomy in the first block matters to the leader and not only to the technology team. Module 6, on Day 3, returns to the subject with governance, risk, the LGPD and regulation.

A Lean lens on adoption

Lean management, as described by Liker in his study of the Toyota Production System, offers three practices that transfer directly to the adoption of AI, because both are concerned with improving a process through the people who run it every day. The first is to go and see where the work happens. Before choosing a tool, the leader observes the decision in its real routine, finding out who prepares the numbers, how long it takes and at which point the information arrives late, and the use case is derived from that observation. The second is standard work. A checking routine for AI outputs, written and shared, allows the team to improve it over time and prevents each person from trusting the tool to a different degree. The third is respect for people, which in this context means that a wrong output can be reported without penalty. Edmondson called this condition psychological safety and showed in her study of work teams that it is associated with learning behaviour, such as asking for help and discussing errors. A team that hides the errors of an AI tool cannot correct the way it uses it.

9. Practice 3: what your team needs from you

The third practice takes twenty minutes: twelve at the tables and eight with the room. The partners at each table discuss three questions, starting from the uses listed in Practice 1 and the roles decomposed in Practice 2, and each table then chooses one answer to share with the room in one minute.

  1. Which decision of your area would you go and observe next week, and what would you look for in its routine?
  2. Which AI output used in your area today has no defined person responsible for checking it?
  3. Which person in your team could become a builder, and what would that person need from you in time, access or permission?

The notes from this discussion are useful in the next hour, since the Caderno asks what the partner and the team already use and what makes the partner suspicious.

10. Caderno do Sócio: the Day 1 section

The Caderno do Sócio is an individual workbook, handed to each participant, in which the content of the three days is applied to the participant's own area. It is not graded, is not handed in and has no right answer. It is filled in at three moments, always in the afternoons: on Day 1 the starting point, on Day 2 one initiative described as a business case and on Day 3 a 30-60-90 action plan with commitments. At the end of Day 3 the partner leaves with a plan written about his or her own area, which continues to be useful after the programme. The Caderno gives one instruction that applies to every section: write little and specifically, because an indicator with a name and a number helps more than a paragraph of intention.

The Day 1 section, titled "My starting point", is filled in during the last hour of this module. The Caderno itself is in Portuguese; the prompts below are its English version, to be used alongside it.

Sharing in pairs

After the individual writing, each partner reads the decision chosen in section 2 to a colleague from another area. The colleague asks three questions to make it more specific: how often the decision is made, who owns it and which number would show that it improved. The roles are then swapped, and each partner rewrites the decision in the Caderno in a more specific form than before. The round takes fifteen minutes, and at the end two partners who volunteer read aloud the decision they chose.

A specific decision is one that a colleague can picture. "Improve commercial decisions" is not specific; "set each month the discount ceiling for off-trade customers in the South region, today based on the previous month's volume" names the decision, the frequency and the current basis, and gives the initiative of Day 2 something to improve.

12. Facilitation notes for the professor

The audience is senior in business and new to data and AI. The programme plan warns that such a class rarely gets lost through difficulty, and that it disengages when content sounds basic or disconnected from decisions. Each concept of the afternoon should therefore be anchored in a decision that the partners recognise, such as setting a discount ceiling, choosing outlets for an investment or approving a promotion.

The time of the Caderno is the first to be sacrificed when a module runs late, and it is the only artefact that the partner takes away. If the earlier blocks overrun, the professor shortens Practice 3 or the discussion of the five levels, and starts the Caderno at 17:00. The practice that matters most for the Caderno is Practice 2, since the decomposition of a role prepares the answers of section 4.

The Maré Alta case appears in the afternoon only as an example. When the class starts to discuss which executive is right, the professor brings the discussion back to the questions a leader would ask, and leaves the analysis to the mornings of Day 2 and Day 3. The questions in section 8 are to remain open during this module.

The professor does not read the Caderno, which is personal. To check progress without evaluating, the professor asks aloud at the end of the hour who wants to read the decision they chose, and walks the room during the individual writing to identify blank pages. A blank page on Day 1 is the case that requires intervention, because a partner who has not opened the Caderno on Day 1 will not build a plan on Day 3. Short questions help a partner who is stuck: which meeting of the month you would cancel if you could, which report you read and do not trust, which number you ask for and never receive.

When presenting the five levels of AI, the professor should state that the scale was reported by the press as an internal tool of one company and is not a technical standard. Questions about which level current tools have reached, or about dates, are better answered by returning to the separation between capability and autonomy, which does not depend on forecasts.
