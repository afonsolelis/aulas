Block 1 · 14:00 – 15:00Structuring data and AI projects

2. Analytical maturity

A data or AI project does not arrive in a neutral environment. It depends on data that someone has to collect and maintain, on people who know how to read its results and on managers willing to change a routine because of them. When these conditions are missing, a technically correct project produces a result that nobody uses. Maturity models were developed to describe these conditions, so that a sponsor can judge which projects an area is able to absorb before committing resources to them.

The five stages of Davenport and Harris

Davenport and Harris (2007) studied organisations that compete on the basis of analytics and described five stages through which an organisation passes. In the first, called analytically impaired, data is missing or of poor quality and decisions rest on experience and intuition. In the second, localised analytics, reports and analyses exist inside functions but are not coordinated, so that sales, finance and marketing may each hold a different number for the same quantity. The third stage, analytical aspirations, is reached when leadership wants analytics to support decisions but data, people and processes are still being built. In the fourth, analytical companies, analytics is used across the business on enterprise data without yet being a distinctive capability, and in the fifth, analytical competitors, it underpins the strategy itself.

The stage matters for project selection because each stage limits the kind of project that can succeed. An area in the second stage usually needs a shared metric and a sponsor who can align two or more functions before it needs a predictive model, while an area in the fourth stage needs portfolio management, that is, the discipline of prioritising, scaling and stopping initiatives. The same organisation may sit at different stages in different areas, which is why the practice of this block asks each partner to locate their own area, and not the company as a whole.

The DELTA elements

Davenport, Harris and Morison (2010) later broke maturity into five elements, summarised by the acronym DELTA. Data refers to information that is accessible, integrated and of known quality. Enterprise refers to an orientation in which data and analysts serve more than one function. Leadership refers to managers who ask for evidence and act on it. Targets refers to the choice of a few decisions on which analytical effort is concentrated, and Analysts refers to the people with the skills to frame and answer business questions. Later versions of the model add technology and analytical techniques to these five elements. The value of the decomposition lies in showing that an area rarely advances on all elements at once, and that the weakest element usually limits the next project. An area with good analysts and fragmented data, for instance, will spend most of any project on data preparation.

Four analytical questions

Davenport and Harris also arrange analytical work by the kind of question it answers, and consultancies such as Gartner present the same idea as a ladder of four steps. Descriptive analytics answers what happened, diagnostic analytics answers why it happened, predictive analytics estimates what is likely to happen and prescriptive analytics recommends what to do. In the Maré Alta case the four steps can be read as four questions: what happened to volume and gross margin in FY26, why the margin fell while volume grew, which outlets are likely to stop buying next quarter and where the R$ 22 million envelope should go to bring the margin back to 48%. Each step depends on the one below it, so a prescriptive question asked in an area whose descriptive numbers are disputed tends to produce a sophisticated answer that nobody trusts.

3. CRISP-DM and the Maré Alta case

The Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP-DM) was published in 2000 by a consortium of companies that included a software vendor, an insurer and an industrial group (Chapman et al., 2000). It describes a data project as a cycle of six phases and remains the reference for analytics projects because it begins and ends with the business, and because it treats the return to an earlier phase as a normal part of the work.

PhaseWhat it producesDecision that falls to the business
1. Business understandingObjective, decision to be improved, success criterion, constraints and planWhat counts as success, and who will use the result
2. Data understandingInventory of sources, first descriptions and a quality reportWhether the available data can answer the question
3. Data preparationCleaned, joined and reconciled data setWhich version is correct when two sources disagree
4. ModellingCandidate models, from simple rules to machine learningWhich errors are more costly for the business
5. EvaluationResult checked against the criterion of phase 1Whether the evidence is good enough to act
6. DeploymentThe result in a routine, with an owner, monitoring and reviewWho changes their routine, and from when

Phases 1, 5 and 6 depend on decisions that the technical team cannot take alone, and this is the main reason why the sponsor of a data project needs to understand the cycle. Phase 3 is usually the longest, since joining sources requires someone with business authority to decide which version of a record is correct. The arrows that return from data understanding to business understanding, and from evaluation to the start, mean that a plan without time for these returns underestimates the project.

The case as an illustration

The first two mornings of the programme went through the first phases of the cycle. On Day 1 the class formulated the business question that the working group pursues for Helena Prado, which corresponds to business understanding, and examined the raw release of the data, which corresponds to data understanding. The curated release delivered for Day 2, with its note on what was changed and what was left as it was, is the product of data preparation. Daniel Okamoto's team built a churn model, and this morning the class evaluated it.

The last phase offers the clearest lesson. According to the case document, the churn model "has been running since the beginning of 2026 without being used in any commercial routine". A model in this situation has the cost of development and operation and produces no value, and the cause of this outcome lies in phase 1, when nobody named the routine, the owner and the decision that would use the model's output. When reading a proposal of their own area, the partner can therefore ask, before anything else, who will do something different on the day the result arrives.

4. Practice 1: where my area stands

The practice takes ten minutes of individual work followed by five minutes of sharing. Each partner writes the answers on the notes page of the Caderno, under Module 4, using the Day 1 section (the recurring decisions of the area and the data the partner does not trust) as a source.

  1. Choose the maturity stage that best describes your area today and write one fact that supports the choice.
  2. Name the DELTA element that most limits your area and the concrete symptom you observe.
  3. Recall the last data or AI initiative of your area and write the CRISP-DM phase in which it stopped or stalled.

In the sharing, two or three partners read the phase in which their last initiative stalled, and the class observes how often the answer falls in phase 1 or phase 6, which are the phases owned by the business.

Block 2 · 15:00 – 15:50Where projects go wrong

5. Five points where projects fail

The technical report of a data project usually describes the model and its metrics, while the causes of failure tend to sit between the business and the data team, in decisions that neither side considers its own. Sculley et al. (2015) showed that in real machine learning systems the model code is a small fraction of the whole, surrounded by data pipelines, configuration, serving and monitoring that accumulate maintenance cost over time. The five points below are the ones a sponsor can examine without technical training, each with a question to ask before the money is committed.

Goal alignment

A project fails on goal alignment when it optimises a metric that is different from the one by which the business will judge it. The three executives of Maré Alta illustrate the problem. Rafael defends the Cooler Programme with the volume growth of programme outlets, Beatriz defends the discount reversal with margin recovered on current volume, and Daniel defends the retention offer with the margin of the outlets that churned and with the accuracy of the model. The board, in turn, has stated its own metric: a credible path back to a gross margin of 48% within an envelope of R$ 22 million. None of the three proposals is wrong because of this, but each one needs a premise that connects its metric to the board's, and that premise is what the sponsor should ask to see.

Data integration

Data integration fails when sources with different keys, units and definitions are joined as if they meant the same thing. In the case, Maré Alta acquired a distributor in the Northeast in 2023, and its outlets are still managed in the LEGACY_NE system, which reaches the corporate platform through a monthly file that the data team has not fully reconciled. Situations like this are common after acquisitions, because the two systems usually differ in the codes used for regions, channels and products, in units of measure and in the period each record refers to, in the keys that identify a customer and in the fields that one system fills and the other leaves empty. The class examined the raw release on Day 1, and the questions for this block are about process: what the data team corrected in LEGACY_NE and on what evidence, what it left as it was because the correction needed a business decision, whether any conclusion of the morning would change if those outlets were analysed separately, and who owns the reconciliation from now on.

Privacy

Privacy fails when personal data is used beyond the purpose for which it was collected or without a legal basis. The Brazilian General Data Protection Law (Lei 13.709/2018, LGPD) requires that personal data be processed for a specified purpose, in a manner compatible with it and limited to what is necessary (art. 6), and on one of the legal bases listed in art. 7. Article 20 gives the data subject the right to request review of decisions taken solely on the basis of automated processing that affect their interests. In a beverage company the point is less remote than it seems, because many small bars and mini-markets are run by individuals, and data about the outlet may also be data about its owner.

Ethics

Ethical problems appear when a model or a rule treats groups differently without anyone having decided so. The most frequent mechanism is inheritance: a model trained on an old classification reproduces it. In the case, the outlet tier was assigned by the sales team in 2023 and has not been reviewed since, so a programme or an offer driven by tier inherits the judgement made at that time. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (NIST, 2023) organises the treatment of such risks in four functions (govern, map, measure and manage) and gives a vocabulary that the sponsor can use with legal and risk teams.

ROI measurement

The return of a project is measured against what would have happened without it. When there is no baseline, meaning the value of the indicator before the project, and no comparison group, meaning a set of customers, outlets or regions that did not receive the intervention, any improvement after the project tends to be attributed to it. The comparison group has to be defined before the start, since choosing it afterwards allows the analysis to pick the group that makes the project look best.

Question the sponsor asks

Which number will the board look at in twelve months, what is its value today, and how will we separate the effect of the project from that of the season, the price or the competition?

6. Practice 2: pre-mortem of a Maré Alta proposal

The pre-mortem, proposed by Klein (2007), asks a team to assume that a project has already failed and to explain why. Because the failure is given, the question shifts from whether the plan is good, which tends to produce agreement, to what caused its failure, which brings out objections that a planning meeting usually silences.

The practice takes fifteen minutes in groups at each table, followed by five minutes of reading aloud. The scenario is the following: it is September 2027, Helena funded one of the three proposals as a project, and the board now considers it a failure. Tables 1 and 4 work on Rafael's extension of the Cooler Programme, tables 2 and 5 on Beatriz's discount reversal and tables 3 and 6 on Daniel's retention offer. Each table writes, under each of the five failure points, the most likely reason for the failure and the early signal that would have shown it in the first 90 days. The reasons are written as statements about the project, such as "the outlets chosen were already the best ones", and leave aside judgements about the people involved. At the end, each table reads the reason it considers most likely.

The guiding questions for the tables are the same ones the class has met since Day 1: how the 400 outlets would be chosen and whether they would resemble the first 236, what the discount estimate assumes about the reaction of volume, and how many of the outlets flagged by the model would have stayed without the offer.

Block 3 · 16:05 – 17:05AI agents

7. AI agents: definition and architecture

The word agent has a long history in computer science. In the classical definition of Russell and Norvig (2020), an agent is anything that perceives its environment and acts upon it. In current products, the term describes a system in which a language model decides, within limits set by its instructions and permissions, which steps to take and which tools to use to reach a goal. Anthropic (2024) draws a useful distinction between workflows, in which the steps are fixed in advance and the model performs one or more of them, and agents, in which the model directs its own process. The distinction matters to a sponsor because the control required grows with the freedom of the system.

The components of an agent

Agent platforms, including Microsoft Copilot Studio, are configured through a similar set of components, described in the platform documentation (Microsoft Learn) and in practitioner guides such as OpenAI (2025). Each component corresponds to a decision that the business owner of the agent has to take, and for this reason the list is also a checklist for any agent proposal that reaches a partner.

8. Requirements and cost drivers

Configuring a simple agent takes minutes, as the demo shows, while running it for a sales force of thousands requires work that is rarely visible in the demo and where most of the cost of an agent sits. The table lists the main drivers and the question a partner can ask the team for each one. Prices and billing units of the platforms change frequently, so current values should be obtained from Ambev's Microsoft licensing team.

DriverWhat determines itQuestion for the team
Licence and consumptionPer-user licences and usage billed per message or credit, which grows with volume and with each generative answer or tool callHow many conversations a month, and what does one conversation cost?
KnowledgeDocuments that are current, versioned and have an owner who updates themWho owns the source, and how does a new version reach the agent?
IntegrationConnectors to ERP, CRM or Teams, authentication and the permissions of the agent's identityWhich systems does it read or write, and under whose permission?
EvaluationA set of test questions with expected answers, run before each changeWhat share of the test answers is correct and cited today?
OperationPeople who receive escalations, approve actions and review the logsWho answers when the agent escalates, and within what time?
Risk and complianceSecurity review, LGPD assessment and the company's AI policyWhich personal data passes through the agent, and who approved it?

Two of these drivers deserve attention because they are usually left out of the first estimate. Evaluation requires a fixed set of questions with expected answers, written by people who know the business, and run again whenever the instructions, the documents or the model change; without it, nobody can say whether a change improved or degraded the agent. Operation requires people: an agent that escalates cases creates a queue, and the queue needs an owner and a response time.

Block 4 · 17:05 – 18:00The AI business case and the Caderno do Sócio

10. Anatomy of an AI business case

The Day 2 section of the Caderno has the same fields as the business case that a sponsor presents to an investment committee. Its credibility depends less on the size of the expected gain than on whether the premise behind the gain can be checked, because a gain whose premise cannot be checked cannot be confirmed or refuted after the project, and therefore offers no basis for scaling or stopping it.

FieldWhat it containsCommon weakness
Problem and decisionThe business problem and the recurring decision that the initiative improvesA technology described in place of a decision
Expected valueGain in time, quality or cost, with the calculation written outA total gain without the number of cases and the value per case
PremiseThe condition that must be true for the gain to happenOmitted because it seems obvious or fragile
KPI and baselineThe indicator that shows the gain and its value todayAn indicator whose current value nobody knows
RisksData, privacy or ethics, adoption by the teamRisks listed without a way to reduce them
EffortPeople, time, systems and what other areas must provideDependence on another area left implicit
Stop conditionThe result that, by a set date, ends the initiativeAbsent, so the initiative continues by inertia

Provost and Fawcett (2013) propose the expected value framework as a way of writing the value of a data-driven action. The value of acting equals the probability that the action works multiplied by the benefit of each success, minus the cost of acting on every case, including the cases in which the action was useless, and minus the cost of running the solution. The framework is useful to a partner because it forces each term to have its own premise: how often the action works, what one success is worth and how many cases will be acted on without need. A business case that presents only the benefit of a success omits the two terms that most often decide whether the initiative pays.

The stop condition is written before the start for a practical reason. Once resources have been spent, the sponsor tends to judge the next step in the light of what has already been invested, and Duke (2022) recommends setting in advance the state and the date that, if reached, end the effort. In the Caderno, the field "under what condition would I stop" carries this function.

11. Worked example: the retention offer

Daniel Okamoto's proposal in the case document serves as an example of a business case whose premise is fragile. The purpose here is to show how such a case is written and examined, and the conclusions about the proposal itself belong to the case closing on Day 3.

As presented, the proposal rests on three facts from the case document: 177 of the 1,150 outlets that were buying in June 2026 placed no order in the following ninety days, these outlets had generated about R$ 9.6 million of gross margin in the previous twelve months, and the churn model is reported as 87% accurate. Daniel proposes a retention offer of R$ 2,000 for every outlet that the model flags, funded by part of the trade budget. Written in the fields of section 10, the expected value depends on four premises: that the outlets flagged are, for the most part, those that would leave; that most of the outlets that leave are flagged; that R$ 2,000 changes the decision of an outlet that intends to leave; and that an outlet kept brings more margin than the offer costs.

None of these premises is answered by the figure of R$ 9.6 million, and the accuracy reported for the model addresses only part of the first two. The questions a sponsor would ask before funding follow from the premises: what accuracy a model that predicts that nobody leaves would achieve, how many flagged outlets would have stayed without the offer, which comparison group would show that the offer caused the retention and what result, by which date, would stop the offer. The evaluation of the model in Module 3 answers part of these questions, and the class keeps its conclusions for the case closing.

The example shows a pattern that partners will meet in their own areas: a large figure for the size of the problem presented as if it were the value of the solution. The value of the solution is always smaller and depends on how well the action is targeted and how often it works, which is why the Caderno asks for the premise separately from the gain.

12. Criteria to prioritise, scale or stop

An initiative passes through successive decisions, and at each one the sponsor asks for a different kind of evidence. The sequence keeps a promising pilot from being scaled before its premise has been tested and keeps a weak initiative from continuing because nobody set the point at which it would end. Davenport and Ronanki (2018) recommend, in the same direction, starting with a portfolio of pilots tied to business processes and scaling only those that demonstrate value.

DecisionEvidence the sponsor asks forTypical criterion
PrioritiseValue, feasibility, available data, a decision owner and a sponsorHigh value with data that already exists and an owner who will change a routine
PilotA premise that can be tested in weeks, a baseline and a comparison groupSmall scope, fixed period and a KPI measured in both groups
ScaleKPI moved against the comparison group, adoption by users, unit cost within the valueThe gain holds in a second region or channel, with an operation owner
StopPremise refuted, KPI flat after the agreed period, cost per case above value, risk without mitigationThe stop condition written in the business case is met

Prioritisation by impact and feasibility across several initiatives, and the data and AI roadmap, are developed in Module 6 on Day 3. In this module the partner applies the criteria to a single initiative, the one written in the Caderno.

The professor of this module passes on to the professors of Day 3 how many partners completed the Day 2 section, the types of initiative chosen, the premises that recurred in the reading aloud and the names of partners who need support to finish before Module 6.

15. Facilitation notes for the professor

The participants are senior partners who decide at a high level every day and are new to data and AI. The class is more likely to disengage because content sounds basic or disconnected from decisions than because it is difficult, so each concept should be anchored in a decision the partners recognise, such as the allocation of trade spend, the choice of outlets for a programme or the approval of a pilot.

The afternoon is strategic, and the case is used only as a shared example. In Blocks 2 and 4, the questions about the three proposals should remain questions: the conclusions of the analysis belong to the class and are closed in Module 5. If a partner asks for the answer, the professor returns the question to the room or refers to the morning's work and the case closing on Day 3. The professor area of the case (see the case materials) contains the facilitator guide for the professors who need it.

Before the session, the professor confirms access to Copilot Studio in the Ambev environment, saves the policy document of section 9 as a file and runs the four test questions once, so that the demo does not depend on configuration in front of the class. The time of the Caderno is the first to be sacrificed when a module runs late, and it is the one to protect: the blocks that can be shortened are the closing discussion of Block 2 and the requirements discussion of Block 3, whose content is in sections 5 and 8 of this material.

There is no grade, and the situation that requires intervention is the blank page.
