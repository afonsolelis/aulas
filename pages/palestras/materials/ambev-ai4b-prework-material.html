About this session

Maré Alta case · Prof. Afonso

Learning objective

By the end of the programme, participants should be able to formulate the business question behind a decision, judge how far an analysis produced with data and AI tools can be trusted, and defend an executive recommendation, using the Maré Alta case as the common thread.

Session strategy

A single guided case runs through the three mornings of the programme. The professors conduct each step: the class formulates its question on Day 1, analyses the extract with Databricks, Genie and Microsoft Copilot on Day 2 and presents a recommendation on Day 3. Nobody writes code, and there is no grade or panel.

Session structure

  1. Before the programme — pre-work, about 85 minutes
  2. Day 1, Module 1 — Problem Framing and Analytical Thinking: the case is presented and the class formulates its business question
  3. Day 2, Module 3 — AI for Data Analytics and Predictive Analysis: the class analyses the extract and discards a conclusion that does not survive checking
  4. Day 3, Module 5 — Visualisation, Storytelling and Decision: the class presents its executive recommendation

On this page

1. Before the three days

This pre-work takes about 85 minutes and prepares the vocabulary that the three days of AI for Business: Data-Driven Decisions take for granted. It asks for no prior knowledge of statistics or programming.

The programme treats data and artificial intelligence from the position of the person who decides. During the three days nobody builds a model or writes a query. The work consists of formulating the question that an analysis has to answer, judging how much an answer can be trusted and choosing between initiatives that compete for the same budget. The readings and videos below were selected because each of them trains one of these three abilities.

The sequence follows the programme. The first block deals with the question, the second with the judgement of evidence and the third with what a generative model does when it answers. The last section introduces the company whose case will run through the three mornings.

2. Framing the problem

Most analyses that disappoint an executive team were technically correct answers to a question that nobody examined. The first morning of the programme is dedicated to this step, in which a situation is turned into a question that names the decision it serves.

Are You Solving the Right Problems?

Thomas Wedell-Wedellsborg, Harvard Business Review, January–February 2017. The article shows, with the example of a slow elevator, how the first formulation of a problem restricts the solutions that anyone will consider, and it proposes seven practices for reframing. While reading, note which of the practices your own team already uses when a request for analysis arrives.

hbr.org/2017/01/are-you-solving-the-right-problems

3. Judging evidence

A recommendation reaches the decision-maker already shaped by the people who prepared it, who selected the data, chose the comparison and believed in the conclusion before presenting it. The two readings of this block deal with the errors that this process introduces and with the questions that expose them.

Before You Make That Big Decision…

Daniel Kahneman, Dan Lovallo and Olivier Sibony, Harvard Business Review, June 2011. The authors start from the distinction between fast, intuitive thinking and slow, deliberate thinking and arrive at twelve questions that an executive can ask of any proposal. The checklist will be used on the first day, so it is worth reading it with a recent decision of your own in mind.

hbr.org/2011/06/the-big-idea-before-you-make-that-big-decision

Beware Spurious Correlations

Harvard Business Review, June 2015. A one-page piece, built on charts, about pairs of series that move together without any relation between them. It prepares the discussion of the second day on what a correlation allows one to conclude.

hbr.org/2015/06/beware-spurious-correlations

4. What a generative model does when it answers

The tools used in the programme answer questions about data in natural language. Using them well depends on understanding that the model produces the most plausible continuation of a text, which is why a wrong answer arrives with the same fluency as a right one.

Large Language Models explained briefly

Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown), 2024, 8 minutes. A visual explanation of how a language model is trained to predict the next word and of what this implies about its answers.

youtube.com/watch?v=LPZh9BOjkQs

Why Large Language Models Hallucinate

IBM Technology, 10 minutes. The video describes the situations in which a model states something false and the practices that reduce the problem, which are the basis of the verification routine of the second day.

youtube.com/watch?v=cfqtFvWOfg0

Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier

Ethan Mollick, One Useful Thing, September 2023. A summary of the experiment conducted with consultants of the Boston Consulting Group, in which the same model improved the work on some tasks and worsened it on others that looked equally easy. The notion of a jagged frontier is useful for deciding where a team may delegate to the tool and where it has to check.

oneusefulthing.org/p/centaurs-and-cyborgs-on-the-jagged

Optional

Two further items are suggested for those who want to go deeper and are not counted in the 85 minutes: the talk 3 ways to spot a bad statistic, by Mona Chalabi (TED, 12 minutes), and the article A Refresher on Statistical Significance, by Amy Gallo (Harvard Business Review, 2016).

5. Maré Alta Beverages: a first look

The case that runs through the three mornings takes place in a fictional company. Maré Alta Beverages brews and distributes beer and non-alcoholic drinks to bars, restaurants, mini-markets, supermarkets and convenience stores in four regions of Brazil. Any resemblance to a real company, brand or outlet is unintended, and all the data was generated for the programme.

In the last two fiscal years the company grew its volume by more than 10% a year and saw its gross margin fall in both. In the same period it launched a programme that installs branded coolers in selected outlets, deepened the discounts it gives to part of its customers and lost about one in seven of the outlets it served. Its chief financial officer has three weeks to take a recommendation on next year's commercial investment to the board, and the three executives she consulted read the same numbers in three different ways.

The full case is handed out on the first morning. Until then, one question is worth carrying into the room: when two colleagues look at the same result and draw opposite conclusions from it, what would you ask each of them before taking a side?
