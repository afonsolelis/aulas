About this session

Module 1 — Problem Framing & Analytical Thinking · 07/12/2026 · Prof. Bryan

Learning objective

By the end of the session, participants should be able to turn a business discussion into one question that names the decision it serves, its deadline, the unknown, the metric and the data it requires, inspect a raw dataset for the quality problems that would distort the answer, and recognise the cognitive biases that shape commercial proposals.

Session strategy

In-person morning that opens the programme and presents the Maré Alta case. Short explanations alternate with four table practices: reading the three executives' positions as claims; formulating the case question with the template 'To decide <decision> by <date>, we need to know <unknown>, measured by <metric>, using <data>', tested against five criteria and settled in a plenary; inspecting the raw release of the data by looking at the tables, with a data issues log and tools kept light (filters, pivot tables, optional read-only Copilot in Excel); and naming the biases that may have shaped each proposal, with the questions to test them on Day 2. The case is guided, not a competition, and the professor narrows the scope instead of giving the answer.

1. Purpose of the morning

The first module installs the method that the rest of the programme depends on: before any analysis is made, the person who decides states which decision is at stake, what is not yet known and which data could resolve it.

The participants of this programme decide every day in their areas, and they are new to data and artificial intelligence. For that reason the module does not teach analysis techniques. It organises, under a data lens, what a partner already does when a proposal reaches the table, and it gives a vocabulary for asking the questions that a data team needs to hear. The programme describes its own aim in these terms: the participant leaves able to demand an analysis, to distrust an output and to prioritise an initiative.

The morning has five blocks and one break. The opening presents the programme and the Maré Alta case. The block on analytical thinking separates skills, mindset and organisational conditions, and introduces four kinds of analytical question. The block on problem framing, the longest of the morning, ends with one question written by the class. After the break, the block on data sources and quality uses the raw release of the case data, and the last block discusses the two modes of thinking described by Kahneman and the biases that most often appear in commercial proposals.

The case is set in Maré Alta Beverages, a fictional brewer and distributor whose volume grew by 11% in FY25 and by 12% in FY26 while its gross margin fell from 50.1% to 46.6% of net revenue. The chief financial officer, Helena Prado, must take one recommendation to the board on how to use a fixed envelope of R$ 22 million for invoice discounts and trade spend in FY27. Three members of the executive committee sent her three positions that read the same data in different ways: Rafael Monteiro wants to extend the Cooler Programme to 400 more outlets, Beatriz Lacerda wants to bring off-trade discounts back to the FY25 level and freeze trade spend, and Daniel Okamoto wants to fund a retention offer for the outlets that a churn model flags. The full text is in the case document, and the tables, the data dictionary and the two releases of the data are on the materials page.

The class plays the working group that Helena convened. The group does not build a model or write a query. Its task is to decide what has to be asked before anyone commits the budget, to judge how far each answer can be trusted and, on the last morning, to defend a recommendation. The case is guided from beginning to end and is not a competition between tables, since its value lies in the questions it forces and not in a race to a solution.

The pre-work asked each participant to bring three notes: which executive they would support today and why, what they would want to know before committing the budget, and what they did not understand in the case document. The opening collects the first note by a show of hands and asks participants to keep it written, because the last block of the morning returns to it when it discusses anchoring and confirmation.

3. Analytical thinking

Skills, mindset and organisations

Analytical thinking is usually discussed as an individual ability, but a decision improves only when three levels are present at the same time. The first level is skill: breaking a problem into parts, comparing like with like, reading every number together with its denominator and its period, and separating what was observed from the interpretation given to it. The second level is mindset, the disposition to treat a first result as a hypothesis, to ask what evidence would change one's opinion and to accept that the answer may contradict the proposal of a colleague or one's own. The third level is organisational, because a skilled person in a company that does not ask for evidence produces analyses that nobody uses.

Davenport and Harris (2007) describe the companies that compete on analytics as those in which senior leaders require evidence for decisions, data are shared across areas instead of guarded by each one, and decisions are reviewed against the results they produced. Their description is useful to a partner because it locates part of the responsibility for analytical quality in leadership itself: the questions a leader asks in a meeting define the kind of analysis the team will prepare for the next one. Provost and Fawcett (2013) give a complementary name to what the manager needs, data-analytic thinking, which is the ability to understand an analysis well enough to ask whether it answers the decision at hand and in which ways it could be wrong, without having to build it.

Four kinds of analytical question

Analytical questions differ in what they ask of the data, and naming the kind of question tells the partner what evidence an answer requires. A descriptive question asks what happened and needs consistent definitions and complete data. A diagnostic question asks why it happened and needs a comparison in which the groups differ only, or mainly, in the suspected cause. A predictive question asks what is likely to happen and needs a model evaluated on cases it did not see, with a metric suited to the decision. A prescriptive question asks what should be done and needs the previous answers together with an explicit criterion of value.

KindWhat it asksBeverage exampleWhat the answer requires
DescriptiveWhat happened?How did can volume in the off-trade evolve over the last three summers?Consistent definitions and complete data for the period
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Why did a region lose share in convenience stores after a price change?A comparison group that differs only in the suspected cause
PredictiveWhat is likely to happen?Which bars are likely to stop ordering in the next quarter?A model evaluated on unseen cases, with the right metric
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Where should next year's trade budget go?The three answers above and an explicit criterion of value

Helena's request is prescriptive, since she must say where the money goes. Each executive, however, supports a prescription with a claim of another kind: a comparison between groups of outlets, an estimate of what a price change would return, a model that anticipates losses. The order in which these claims are tested depends on the question the class writes in the next block, which is why the reading of the three positions comes first.

4. Problem framing

From business question to decision and data

A business question becomes useful when it is attached to a decision that someone will take, because only then can the group say which unknown matters and which data would resolve it. Questions that are not attached to a decision tend to produce analyses that are interesting and inconsequential, since nothing in them indicates when the analysis is finished or what it should change. The chain below, adapted from the discussion of business problems in Provost and Fawcett (2013, ch. 2) and from the business understanding phase of CRISP-DM 1.0 (Chapman et al., 2000), makes the attachment explicit in five steps.

  1. Decision: which choice will be made, by whom, by when, and among which alternatives.
  2. Unknown: what the decision-maker does not know today and that would change the choice if it were known.
  3. Metric: how the unknown is measured, with the indicator, its unit, its period and the comparison against which it is read.
  4. Data: which tables and columns hold the metric, at what level of detail, and with which known gaps.
  5. Action: what the decision-maker would do for each plausible answer, written before the analysis begins.

The fifth step protects the group against a question whose every answer leads to the same action. When that happens, the analysis has no value for the decision, however well it is executed, and the group should go back to the second step and look for an unknown that actually separates the alternatives.

The template

The template below forces the five elements into a single sentence, so that a missing element appears as an empty slot rather than hiding inside a long paragraph.

To decide <decision> by <date>, we need to know <unknown>, measured by <metric>, using <data>.

A worked example outside the case shows the template in use. A distributor considers whether to keep a second weekly delivery to mini-markets in the interior of a state. The question reads: to decide whether to keep the second weekly delivery to mini-markets in the interior by March 2027, we need to know how much volume these outlets would lose with a single weekly delivery, measured by the change in monthly hectolitres per outlet against comparable outlets already on one delivery, using two years of invoices by outlet and the route calendar. The decision has an owner and a date, the unknown is the only fact that would change the choice, the metric names its comparison group, and the data exist in systems the company already runs. The example is deliberately taken from logistics: the question of the case is written by the class, and neither this material nor the deck contains it.

Narrowing a question that is too broad

Broad questions are the most frequent failure of this block, because they feel safe and cannot be refuted. A question that is too broad cannot be answered with one extract in one morning, and a weak question on Day 1 compromises the two following days. The group narrows it by fixing one element of the template at a time, as in the sequence below, again outside the case.

StepQuestionWhat was fixed
Too broadHow do we reduce the cost of returnable bottles?Nothing: no decision, no date, no metric
Decision fixedShould we raise the deposit charged to bars on returnable crates in the 2027 price list?A choice with an owner and a moment
Unknown fixedWould bars return crates faster if the deposit were higher?The fact that would change the choice
Decision-readyTo decide whether to raise the crate deposit before the 2027 price list is published, we need to know whether return times differ between bars paying different deposits, measured by the share of crates returned within 30 days at each deposit level, using crate movement records by outlet.Metric, comparison and data

At each step the group asks two things: which element of the template is still open, and which of the proposals on the table the answer would confirm or reject. When the group cannot answer the second, the question is still too broad, or it is attached to no proposal at all.

Five tests for the question

Before reading its draft to the class, each table applies the five tests and rewrites the element that fails, instead of adding words to the sentence. A question that passes all five is not necessarily the best one, but it can be analysed on Day 2 with the extract and answered on Day 3 in front of the board.

TestThe question passes whenA sign that it fails
DecisionIt names a choice that Helena takes to the board within the R$ 22 million envelopeIt describes a topic, such as "understand the margin"
ConsequenceDifferent answers lead to different allocations of the budgetEvery plausible answer leads to the same action
MeasurabilityThe unknown has a metric, a period and a comparisonIt uses words such as "impact" or "effectiveness" without a measure
DataThe five tables of the extract can, in principle, produce the metricIt requires data the company does not have, such as competitor prices

Practice: the class formulates the case question

The practice takes 45 of the 60 minutes of the block and has four steps. In the first, for eight minutes, each participant writes one question with the template, starting from the pre-work note on what they would want to know before committing the budget. In the second, for twelve minutes, each table chooses or merges one question and applies the five tests. In the third, for fifteen minutes, each table reads its question, and the class marks which decision each serves and which proposal it would put to the test. In the fourth, for ten minutes, the class settles one question, narrowing it if necessary, and the professor records it in the five fields of the deck.

The question does not need to favour any of the executives. It needs to identify what Helena must know before she chooses, and it may cover more than one proposal only if a single metric can test them. A question that covers all three proposals with three different metrics is, in practice, three questions, and the class should choose the one whose answer would move the largest part of the budget or the one whose evidence is most disputed.

5. Data sources, governance and quality

Where the data of a commercial decision come from

Every number that reaches a partner has passed through systems, files and people, and each passage can change it. In a beverage company the data of a commercial decision usually come from four kinds of source. Transactional systems, such as the corporate ERP, record invoices, discounts and costs at the moment of sale. Inherited systems, typical after an acquisition, keep their own codes and conventions and often reach the corporate platform through periodic files. External sources, such as indicators bought from a provider, have a coverage that the company does not control. Derived data, such as the scores of a model, inherit the quality of all the inputs that produced them. The Maré Alta extract contains the four kinds: outlets managed in ERP_CORE and in LEGACY_NE, the system of the distributor acquired in the Northeast in 2023, an income index bought from a provider, and the scores of the churn model.

Data governance is the set of decisions about who owns each source, who may change it, which definitions are official and which version of a table is the one used for decisions. The data owner, usually a business area, decides what a field means and what counts as correct. The data steward or the data team applies the rules that follow from those decisions, and the analyst reports what could not be corrected and how it affects the result. When these roles are not defined, the same indicator is calculated in different ways by different areas, and meetings are spent reconciling numbers instead of discussing decisions.

Data platforms such as Databricks commonly keep the data in layers, with a raw layer that stores the data as received from the sources and one or more curated layers in which the data team has applied the corrections. The case follows the same logic. The raw release is the extract as it left the source systems, and it is the one used this morning. The curated release, delivered on Day 2 with a release note, is the version that the data team produced after the working group reviewed the raw one. Using the raw release on Day 1 lets the class see what curation has to decide, and which of those decisions belong to the business rather than to the data team.

Dimensions of data quality

Data are of good quality when they are fit for the decision they serve, a definition that Wang and Strong (1996) established from the point of view of the people who use the data and that DAMA-DMBOK (DAMA International, 2017) organises into dimensions. The six dimensions below are those that can be checked by looking at the tables, without code.

DimensionQuestion to askHow to look at it without code
CompletenessAre values missing, and are they missing at random or concentrated in one group?Filter blanks in each column and compare their share by source system or region
ConsistencyIs the same thing written the same way everywhere?List the distinct values of each text column and compare them with the data dictionary
ValidityAre values within the range and the unit that the dictionary states?Sort numeric columns and read the largest, the smallest and the negative values
UniquenessDoes each key appear only once?Count rows by month and region and look for a month that jumps without a business reason
IntegrityDoes every key in one table exist in the table it refers to?Check that the identifiers used in the other tables appear in pos
TimelinessDo the data cover the period the question needs?Read the first and last month and the snapshot date of each table

The question of the class decides which dimensions matter most. Missing values in a column that the question does not use are a note for the data team, whereas a problem in the column that holds the metric can change the answer, and the class should record it as such.

Practice: inspecting the raw release

The practice takes 30 minutes. Each table takes the tables of the extract on which its question depends most, so that the five tables are covered across the room. For fifteen minutes the table opens the raw workbook, available on the materials page, applies filters, sorts columns and, where useful, builds a pivot table, following the six dimensions. Nothing in the data is changed. For five minutes the table records each problem in the issues log below, and in the last seven minutes each table reads its most serious issue while the class decides which issues need a business decision.

The tools stay light in this module on purpose. Filters, sorting and pivot tables in Excel are enough to see the problems, and Module 3 is where Databricks, Genie and Copilot are used with prompt engineering on the curated release. Participants who want to try Copilot in Excel today may use it for read-only questions, such as the ones below, which ask it to describe the data without changing them. Copilot in Excel works on data formatted as an Excel table, which is how the workbook is delivered.

Copilot in Excel · read-only · distinct values
In table pos, list the distinct values of the column region and the number of rows for each. Do not change, sort or clean the data. If a value looks like a variant of another, say so and do not merge them.
Copilot in Excel · read-only · missing values
In table pos, count the empty cells in each column, and show the count again split by region and by source_system. Do not fill, delete or change any value.
Copilot in Excel · read-only · monthly totals
Using table sales_monthly, show the total of volume_hl by month and by the region of the point of sale. The fiscal year runs from October to September. Point out any month that differs strongly from the months around it, without explaining why and without changing the data.

Any answer from Copilot is a lead to check by looking at the table, and not a finding in itself. Module 3 discusses in depth how to verify outputs of AI tools, and the rule for today is simpler: a problem enters the log only after someone at the table has seen it in the rows.

The data issues log

The log turns observations into requests that the data team can act on, and it separates what can be corrected by rule from what depends on a decision of the business. A problem is correctable by rule when the correct value is known without judgement, as when the same category is written in two ways. A problem needs a business decision when its treatment changes the result and there is no single correct answer, as when the group must choose whether to exclude certain records or how to treat values that do not exist in the source.

6. Heuristics and biases

System 1 and System 2

Kahneman (2011) describes two modes of thinking. System 1 is fast, associative and effortless; it builds a coherent story from the information available and does not signal what is missing, a property Kahneman summarises in the expression "what you see is all there is". System 2 is slow, effortful and capable of checking, and it is activated when the stakes are made explicit or when a procedure requires it. Most biases studied in the literature appear when a decision that needs System 2 is taken by System 1, and the practical consequence for a leader is that procedures, such as a written question or a checklist, are more reliable than the intention to be careful.

The heuristics behind these biases are not defects to be eliminated. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) showed that the same shortcuts that usually produce good judgements under uncertainty also produce systematic and predictable errors, which is why the errors can be anticipated and countered by asking the right question at the right moment.

Typical and ideal decision process

Bazerman and Moore (2013, ch. 1) describe a rational decision process in six steps: define the problem, identify the criteria, weight the criteria, generate alternatives, rate each alternative on each criterion and compute the best decision. Real meetings rarely follow it. The table contrasts what usually happens in a commercial meeting with the ideal process, and the contrast explains why the framing work of this morning comes before any analysis.

Typical process in a commercial meetingIdeal process
The first proposal presented sets the reference for the discussionThe problem is defined before any proposal is discussed
Evidence is gathered to support the preferred optionCriteria are set before the alternatives are evaluated
Alternatives are compared by the confidence of their sponsorsSeveral alternatives are generated and compared on the same criteria
The decision is not reviewed against resultsThe decision states in advance what result would reverse it

Five biases that shape commercial proposals

Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek and weigh more heavily the evidence that supports what one already believes (Nickerson, 1998); in a commercial meeting it appears when only the outlets in which an initiative worked are presented. Anchoring is the pull that a first number exerts on later estimates (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974); it appears when next year's budget is discussed as a variation of this year's figure. Survivorship bias arises when only the cases that remained are observed, as in the analysis of returning aircraft discussed by Mangel and Samaniego (1984); in commerce it appears when success factors are read from the customers still active, without the ones that left. Availability is the tendency to judge as frequent what is recent or vivid (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974), as when the loss of one large customer last month dominates the plan. Overconfidence is the overestimation of the precision of one's own judgements (Kahneman, 2011), which appears when a single headline metric is presented as settled.

BiasQuestion that brings System 2 back into the meeting
ConfirmationWhat result would show that the proposal is wrong, and has anyone looked for it?
AnchoringWould we reach the same number if we started from zero?
SurvivorshipWho is missing from the data, and why?
AvailabilityHow often does it happen, counted over the full base?
OverconfidenceCompared with what, and how could this metric mislead?

Kahneman, Lovallo and Sibony (2011) propose that the person who approves a recommendation, rather than the team that prepared it, ask a short list of questions before deciding: whether the team may be motivated by self-interest or attached to its proposal, whether there were dissenting opinions, whether credible alternatives were considered, and where the numbers came from. The list fits the situation of Helena, who receives three recommendations prepared by three sponsors.

Practice: biases in the three positions

The practice takes twenty minutes. Each table takes one executive, names the bias that could have shaped how the proposal was built and writes the question that would test it with the data on Day 2. The guiding questions below help the tables start, and none of them presupposes the answer.

  • For the Cooler Programme, ask how the outlets of the programme were chosen and how the 400 new ones would be chosen, and ask what the two groups of outlets looked like before the coolers arrived.
  • For the discount proposal, ask what the estimate of R$ 3.3 million assumes about the volume of the off-trade when discounts change, and which past period of the extract could show how volume responded.
  • For the retention offer, ask what accuracy a model that predicts that no outlet leaves would have, and how many of the 177 outlets that stopped buying the model actually flagged.

The block closes by returning to the show of hands of the opening. Each participant checks whether the executive they supported at 09:00 received the same scrutiny as the other two, which is a direct way of observing anchoring and confirmation in one's own judgement. The questions written by the tables go to Module 3 together with the question of the class.

7. Hand-off to Module 2 and Module 3

The morning produces three outputs that travel to the next professors: the question of the class, recorded in the five fields of the template; the data issues log of each table; and the questions written in the biases block. The professor collects the logs and the questions before the class leaves and forwards them, together with the question, to Prof. Afonso.

In the afternoon, Module 2, with Prof. Kim Silvestre, does not advance the case. It discusses generative AI applied to business, the collaboration between people and AI and the role of leadership, and it opens the Caderno do Sócio. In the Day 1 section of the Caderno, each partner lists three to five recurring decisions of the own area, chooses one where the right information at the right time would change the decision, and describes the data the area generates, who consumes them, what the partner does not trust and what is requested and never arrives. The framing template of section 4 applies directly to the chosen decision, and the quality dimensions of section 5 help to answer what the partner does not trust.

On the morning of Day 2, Module 3, with Prof. Afonso, opens with a fifteen-minute recap of the question formulated today. The module delivers the curated release with its release note, discusses statistical reasoning for decisions and the evaluation of predictive models with a focus on interpretation, and uses Databricks, Genie and Copilot with prompt engineering on the case data. By the end of Day 2 the class is expected to have analysed the extract, interpreted the results and set aside at least one conclusion that did not survive checking.

8. Facilitation notes for the professor

The problem framing block is the most important of the morning, and the timing of the other blocks should give way to it if the class needs more time. The opening and the analytical thinking block can be shortened by five minutes each without loss, whereas a question that is still too broad at 11:15 compromises Day 2 and Day 3. If the block overruns, the time of the biases practice can be reduced to fifteen minutes.

When the class stalls on the question, the plan of the programme recommends reducing the scope of the case instead of giving the answer. In practice, the professor fixes one element of the template for the class, such as one proposal, one channel or one region, and asks the group to complete the others. The questions that help at this point are which of the three proposals the answer would change, what Helena would do differently for each possible answer, and whether the five tables of the extract could, in principle, produce the metric. The professor does not propose a question of their own and does not indicate which executive is right.

The data quality block should remain light on tools. This was raised in the preparation of the programme because Module 3 covers Databricks, Genie and Copilot in depth, and repeating the tool practice on Day 1 would take time from the framing work and anticipate the following module. Filters, sorting and pivot tables are sufficient for the participants to find the problems by looking at the rows; Copilot in Excel is optional and limited to read-only questions. If a table asks for Databricks or Genie, the professor notes the request for Module 3. Confirming in advance that each participant can open the raw workbook avoids losing the practice to access problems.

The biases block should not be read as an accusation against any of the three executives. Each executive in the case has a reasonable argument, and the purpose is to show that any proposal, including the one each participant preferred at the opening, can be shaped by the same mechanisms. The guiding questions of section 6 are to be asked as they stand; the professor does not answer them, since they are answered by the class with the curated data on Day 2.

No grade is given in the programme. The professor checks progress by asking aloud, at the end of each practice, who wants to read the question they wrote or the issue they found. A blank page is the situation that requires intervention. Before leaving, the professor records the question of the class, collects the issues logs and the bias questions, and sends them to Prof. Afonso, so that the recap of Module 3 starts from what was actually decided.
