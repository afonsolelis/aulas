2. Choosing the chart by the business question

A chart is a device that makes one comparison easy for the reader, and for that reason the choice of chart begins with the question the chart must answer. When the question is not stated, the author tends to choose the chart the tool offers first or the one that looks most elaborate, and the reader then has to work out which comparison matters. Five types of question cover most of what reaches an executive committee, and each of them corresponds to a family of charts.

Question typeWhat the reader comparesUsual chartMaré Alta example
ComparisonThe size of items at one momentBar chart sorted by value, axis starting at zeroVolume by region in FY26
TrendThe change of a measure over timeLine chart with time on the horizontal axisGross margin from FY24 to FY26
CompositionThe parts of a totalStacked bar or a single 100% barHow the FY26 envelope was spent
DistributionHow values spread across unitsHistogram or box plotVolume growth across the 1,150 outlets
RelationshipWhether two measures move togetherScatter plot, coloured by segmentDiscount depth against volume by outlet

The correspondence in the table rests on experimental evidence about how people read graphs. Cleveland and McGill (1984) asked readers to estimate quantities encoded in different ways and found that judgements of position along a common scale were the most accurate, followed by length, while angle, area and colour intensity produced larger errors. Bars and lines encode values by position and length, which explains why they serve a decision better than pies, gauges and bubble charts, whose values are read through angle or area. Tufte (2001) reaches a related principle from another direction, by asking that the visual size of an effect be proportional to the size of the effect in the data.

Trend: an honest axis for the margin

The case table gives three values of gross margin, 50.1% in FY24, 48.4% in FY25 and 46.6% in FY26, and the question they answer is a trend. Figure 1 draws the same three values in two ways. In the first, the values are bars and the vertical axis starts at 46%, so the FY26 bar is about one seventh of the FY24 bar, although the margin fell by 3.5 percentage points. Because a bar is read by its length, its axis must start at zero, and a bar chart with a truncated axis exaggerates any change. In the second, the values are a line whose axis starts at 40% and is labelled, which is acceptable because a line is read by its slope and position rather than by its length. The dashed line places the 48% expected by the board on the same scale, so the reader sees both the fall and the distance to the target.

Comparison: two measures on different scales

The argument of the case document starts from two measures that moved in opposite directions: volume grew from 292 to 366 thousand hectolitres while gross margin fell. A common way to show this is a chart with two vertical axes, bars of volume on the left and a line of margin on the right. The author of such a chart chooses both scales, and therefore chooses where the line crosses the bars, so the visual impression of a relationship between the two measures is produced by the design and not by the data. A safer alternative is to index every series to the same base, FY24 = 100. Indexed in this way, volume reaches 125 in FY26, net revenue reaches 134, and gross profit, obtained by multiplying net revenue by gross margin in each year, reaches 125. The indexed chart shows on one scale how much each measure grew, and it leaves open the question of why, which belongs to the analysis of the groups.

Composition, distribution and relationship

A composition question asks what a total is made of. The case document states that the company invested R$ 22.2 million in invoice discounts and trade spend in FY26, that trade spend was R$ 5.2 million and that the Cooler Programme cost R$ 2.7 million, so invoice discounts account for about R$ 17.0 million of the envelope and trade spend outside the programme for about R$ 2.5 million. A single horizontal bar divided into those three parts, with the values written beside each part, answers the question directly. A pie chart is the usual alternative, and it becomes misleading when it is used for something that is not a set of parts, such as trade spend split by year, since the order of time disappears around the circle and the reader is left comparing angles.

Distribution and relationship questions require data by outlet, which the five tables of the extract provide. A histogram of volume growth per outlet shows whether an average describes the typical outlet or is pulled up by a few outlets with very high growth, which matters whenever an average is used to justify a budget. A scatter plot shows whether two measures move together, and it should be coloured by segment, because a relationship that appears across the whole set of outlets may weaken or vanish inside each segment when the segments differ from the start. In both cases the chart does not settle a causal question by itself. It shows the group where to look, and the reading of correlation and causation from Module 3 applies to what it shows.

The title states the message

The title of an analytical chart in an executive document is a sentence that states what the reader should see, such as "Gross margin fell 3.5 points in two years while volume grew by a quarter", rather than a label such as "Gross margin, FY24–FY26". Knaflic (2015) recommends this practice because the title is read first and frames everything else on the chart. The practice also works as a test for the author: if the group cannot write a title that the data support, the chart is not yet ready to be shown.

3. Building and checking the chart with Copilot in Excel

The workbook of the curated release has one sheet per table, already formatted as an Excel table, which is what Copilot in Excel requires. Copilot answers better when the prompt names the table, the period, the metric and the grouping, and the fiscal year of Maré Alta, which runs from October to September, has to be stated in the prompt because a tool will otherwise assume the calendar year. The first prompt below builds the trend chart of gross margin, and the same structure serves other questions once the metric, the grouping and the chart type are changed.

Prompt 1 · build the chart
In the table sales_monthly, add a column fiscal_year: months from October to September belong to the fiscal year that ends in that September (October 2025 to September 2026 is FY26).
Then create a PivotTable with fiscal_year in rows and the sums of net_revenue_brl and cogs_brl.
Add gross margin % = (net_revenue_brl − cogs_brl) / net_revenue_brl.
Insert a line chart of gross margin % by fiscal year, with the vertical axis labelled in %.
Write the chart title as one sentence that states what the line shows.

The second prompt asks Copilot to show the numbers behind the chart. Since Copilot can apply a formula different from the one requested or misread the fiscal year, the group compares the totals it returns with values it already knows, such as the gross margin of the case table, before the chart enters the recommendation. This is the verification habit of the last block of Module 3 applied to a chart.

Prompt 2 · check before using
Show in a table the totals of net revenue and COGS for each fiscal year and the formula used for gross margin %, so that I can compare them with the case document.

For other question types, the instruction about the chart changes. A comparison asks for a bar chart sorted by value with the axis starting at zero; a composition asks for one stacked bar with the values written on each part; a relationship asks for a scatter plot with one point per outlet, coloured by tier. The sales table has about 100,000 rows, so a question asked on a PivotTable or on one of the smaller tables is answered faster and more reliably than one asked on the full table.

Practice 1 · the chart for the group's question (15 min)

  1. Name the type of question behind the recommendation the group is preparing: comparison, trend, composition, distribution or relationship.
  2. Sketch the chart by hand with its axes, units and period, before opening any tool.
  3. Adapt Prompt 1 to the metric and grouping of the group and run it in Copilot on the curated workbook; then run Prompt 2.
  4. Write the title as a sentence and check that the data support it.

Before moving on, each group answers two questions about its chart: which comparison the chart makes easy for the reader, and which conclusion a reader could draw from it that the analysis of Day 2 does not support.

4. Strategic and tactical dashboards

Few (2006) defines a dashboard as a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives, arranged on a single screen so that it can be monitored at a glance. The definition implies a reader and an objective, and Eckerson (2010) derives from it three types of dashboard that differ in audience, rhythm and level of detail. Operational dashboards serve the people who run the daily activity and show what needs attention today. Tactical dashboards serve the managers of a function and help them find where a deviation comes from and correct it within weeks. Strategic dashboards serve the executive committee and the board and show whether the strategy and the budget are on course over months and quarters.

TypeWho reads itRhythmDecision it supportsMaré Alta example
StrategicBoard, CFO, executive committeeMonthly or quarterlyWhether the strategy and the budget are on courseGross margin against the 48% path; use of the R$ 22 million envelope
TacticalHeads of sales, revenue management, dataWeeklyWhich programme, region or channel needs correctionDiscount depth by channel; results of the outlets that received an action
OperationalSupervisors and sales representativesDailyWhat to do today at which outletOutlets without orders in the last 30 days, by route

The distinction has a practical consequence for the partners. A strategic dashboard carries few indicators, each with its target and its trend, because the board reads it to decide whether to intervene, and additional detail at that level delays the decision without improving it. A tactical dashboard, by contrast, must allow the reader to break an indicator down by region, channel, tier or product, since its reader has to locate a deviation before acting on it. When a single screen is asked to serve both audiences, it usually ends up with too many indicators for the board and too little breakdown for the manager.

5. KPIs: indicators tied to a decision

A key performance indicator is an indicator that the company has tied to a decision and has defined with enough precision for two analysts, working on the same data, to arrive at the same number. The precision matters because most disputes about numbers in an executive committee are disputes about definitions. In the case, gross margin is defined in the data dictionary as net revenue minus cost of goods sold and excludes trade spend, which means that a recommendation that moves money from invoice discounts to trade spend changes the reported margin even if the commercial investment stays the same. A KPI therefore carries a small set of fields, illustrated below with the margin of the case.

Indicators also differ in the moment at which they move. Gross margin is a lagging indicator, which confirms a result once the period is over. Discount depth as a percentage of gross revenue, the number of active outlets per month or the share of outlets reached by an action are leading indicators, because they move earlier and allow the company to correct course while the fiscal year is still open. Kaplan and Norton (1992) built the balanced scorecard on this combination of outcome measures and the drivers that precede them.

Every target also changes the behaviour of the people measured by it. Strathern (1997), generalising an observation by Goodhart, noted that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure, because people learn to improve the number by means that do not improve what the number was meant to show. For each KPI the group proposes, it is worth asking how a team could meet the target in a way the company would not want. A discount target, for example, can be met by shifting the same concession from the invoice to another line of spend.

6. OKRs: objectives and key results

Objectives and key results are a goal-setting method that Andy Grove applied at Intel and described in High Output Management (1983), and that John Doerr later took to Google and set out in Measure What Matters (2018). An objective describes what the area wants to achieve in the period, in words that are significant, concrete and oriented to action, and it is not measured directly. The key results, usually three to five per objective, specify how achievement will be verified: each one has a number, a deadline and a source, so that at the end of the period it has been met or not met without discussion. Doerr summarises the format in the sentence "I will (objective) as measured by (key results)".

KPIs and OKRs serve different purposes and are used together. KPIs monitor the health of the business continuously, while an OKR concentrates the effort of a team on a change during one period. A KPI that drifts away from its target is often the reason an OKR is written. Doerr also distinguishes committed OKRs, which the area is expected to meet in full, from aspirational ones, which are deliberately set above what the area expects to reach, and a board reading an OKR needs to know which of the two it is reading.

Example outside the case

A beverage distributor that wants to bring the service level of an acquired region up to that of its core region could write the objective "Make deliveries in the Northeast as reliable as in the Southeast before the summer season", with three key results: on-time, in-full deliveries in the Northeast rise from their current value to the Southeast level by March; outlets of the acquired distributor managed in the corporate system reach 100% by January; returns caused by delivery errors fall by half against the same quarter of the previous year.

Practice 2 · the board view of the FY27 decision (20 min)

Whatever the group recommends, the board will want to follow the decision month by month, and choosing the indicators now forces the group to say what success would look like. Each group writes:

  • three KPIs for Helena's strategic dashboard, each with definition, source, current value, target and owner;
  • one leading indicator that would show within 90 days whether the recommendation is working;
  • one OKR for FY27 that the recommended initiative would serve, with no more than three key results.

To test the choice, the group asks whether each indicator can be computed from the five tables of the extract or needs data the company does not yet have, whether it would move if the recommendation failed or would move for other reasons, and how a team could meet the target without improving the margin. Two groups read their KPIs aloud at the end of the practice.

7. The Pyramid Principle

Barbara Minto developed the Pyramid Principle while training consultants at McKinsey, and published it as The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking. The principle starts from an observation about readers: a person understands a set of ideas faster when the idea that summarises them is presented first, because the reader can then place each following idea in relation to it, instead of holding all of them in memory while waiting for the conclusion. Analysts tend to write in the opposite order, in which the work was done, from the data to the findings and finally to the recommendation. Minto's proposal is to reverse that order in the document while keeping the analysis intact underneath.

The pyramid has three levels of statements. At the top is the governing thought, the single statement the reader must retain, written as a complete sentence that contains the recommendation. Immediately below is the key line, a small group of statements that support the governing thought, which may be reasons, steps or options. Below each statement of the key line lie the data, analyses and charts that support it. Two kinds of logic hold the structure together. The vertical logic is a dialogue between author and reader: each statement raises a question in the reader's mind (why, how, which), and the level below answers it. The horizontal logic governs each group of statements: they must be of the same kind, must not overlap, should together cover what the statement above requires, and must follow an order that can be explained, whether in time, in structure or in importance. Deductive chains, in which each statement follows from the previous one, are kept short, because a board that rejects one premise rejects the whole chain.

The consequence for an executive recommendation is that a reader can stop at any level and still leave with the decision. A board member who reads only the governing thought knows what is proposed; one who reads the key line knows why; one who descends to the evidence can check where each number comes from. Knaflic (2015) applies the same discipline to slides, asking the author to write the main idea in one sentence before building any visual, and to write slide titles as statements so that someone reading only the titles can repeat the argument.

8. SCQA and the skeleton of the Maré Alta recommendation

Minto opens a document with an introduction in four parts, known by the initials SCQA. The situation is a fact the reader already knows and does not dispute, which sets the context. The complication is what changed or went wrong in that situation and makes a decision necessary. The question is the one the complication raises in the reader's mind, often left implicit. The answer is the governing thought, which the rest of the document supports. The introduction works because the reader agrees with each step before reaching the answer, and the answer therefore arrives as the resolution of a question the reader already holds.

Example outside the case

Situation: returnable bottles carry most of the on-trade volume, and their float is planned once a year. Complication: two regions ran short of bottles in the last summer peak, while a third held stock above plan. Question: how should the float be allocated before the next peak? Answer: rebalance the float between regions by December and review it monthly during the peak, because total stock covered demand and the shortage came from its allocation.

Its slots are left empty because the case admits more than one defensible recommendation: the three executive proposals may appear as options considered, as parts of the answer or as alternatives set aside, and a group may recommend a combination or a sequence, provided it fits the envelope and the constraints of the case document. In every case the page explains the reason for the choice.

Practice 3 · governing thought and SCQA (25 min)

  1. Write the governing thought in one sentence, with what is recommended and the main reason. A governing thought that does not fit in one sentence usually means that the group has not yet made the decision.
  2. Write the situation, the complication and the question so that the question leads to that answer and the situation contains nothing Helena would dispute.
  3. List the statements of the key line, check that they do not overlap and order them by their importance to the board.
  4. In the last five minutes, read the top of the pyramid aloud to the group beside you and ask which question it raises first.

9. Case closing: from insight to executive recommendation

The last 65 minutes of the morning are reserved in full for the closing of the case, as the programme plan requires, because it is the block in which the question of Day 1, the analysis of Day 2 and the narrative built during the morning become a recommendation that can be defended. The work corresponds to the role the case document assigns to the working group: to decide what had to be asked before anyone committed the budget, to judge how far each answer can be trusted and to defend a recommendation in front of people who will challenge it.

What each group delivers

Each group delivers one page and one chart. The page follows the order of the pyramid: the SCQA introduction and the governing thought, the key line with the evidence of Day 2 and its source, the conclusion the group discarded and the reason, the KPI that will show whether the decision is working, and the condition under which the group would change its mind. The chart is the one prepared in Practice 1, chosen by the type of question and titled with its message. The constraints of the case document remain in force: an envelope of R$ 22 million for invoice discounts and trade spend, a credible path back to a gross margin of 48%, installed coolers staying in place for the year, and a decision on off-trade discounts before the January renegotiation with the supermarket chains.

Before the round, each group checks three things: whether the first sentence of the page contains the decision, whether the chart answers the type of question the group classified, and whether each number on the page can be traced to a table, a release and a period.

The challenge round

The group that plays Helena and the board starts from the question Helena wrote in her message to the working group, why do you believe it, and adds at most one more question. The list below offers questions that test the reasoning behind any of the possible recommendations; the board group chooses those that fit the page in front of it and may write its own.

  • Why do you believe it, and which evidence would you show first?
  • How would the 400 additional outlets be chosen, and with which outlets would you compare them?
  • What accuracy would a model that predicts that nobody leaves have?
  • What does the margin estimate assume about how volume reacts to a change in discounts?
  • Does the comparison hold among outlets of the same tier?
  • Which data quality problem found on Day 1 could still affect this number?
  • What result in 90 days would make you change your mind?

The round is not a competition and carries no grade. Two groups that reach different decisions can both have reasoned well, and the class compares how each one connected question, evidence and decision, and which challenges each page could and could not answer.

10. Hand-off to Module 6

The afternoon of Day 3 belongs to Prof. Egon and to Module 6, Leading an AI-Driven Organization, which covers AI governance, risk and regulation, the prioritisation of initiatives and the last section of the Caderno do Sócio, the 30-60-90 action plan of each partner's area. The case does not advance in the afternoon, but it remains available as a shared example, and its governance angle connects naturally with the first block of the module.

Three situations of the case raise questions of governance that the partners will have discussed from the analytical side during the morning. The first is the use of a model score to allocate money to outlets, which requires someone to authorise the use, to define who reviews the outcome and to decide when the model is stopped. The second is a churn model that has run since the beginning of 2026 without being used in any commercial routine, which raises the question of who owns a model once it is built. The third is an extract assembled from two systems that have not been fully reconciled, which raises the question of who answers for the quality of data that reaches the executive committee. The questions recorded during the challenge round can serve as examples for that discussion.

On the side of the Caderno, Day 1 mapped the recurring decisions of each area and Day 2 described one initiative of the area as a business case with value, premise, KPI and stop condition. The KPI fields of Practice 2 follow the same logic, and partners can reuse them when they write the milestones and evidence of the 30-60-90 plan in the afternoon.

11. Facilitation notes for the professor

The plan of the programme identifies the main risk of the morning as losing the closing time to the earlier blocks, and the agenda protects the 65 minutes accordingly. If Block 1 or Block 2 runs late, the practice of that block is shortened before the closing is touched. Practice 3 can also be merged into the first minutes of the closing if needed, since the skeleton of Practice 3 is the starting point of the page.

The groups formed on Day 1 are kept, so that each group closes the question it formulated. With 20 to 30 participants, four or five groups of five or six people fit the challenge round in 25 minutes; with four groups, each presentation can take six minutes instead of five. The order of the round is a circle, in which group 1 presents to group 2, group 2 to group 3, and the last group to group 1, so that every group both presents and challenges once.

The professor does not indicate a preferred recommendation at any moment, either during the group work or in the round. When a group is stuck, the intervention follows the guidance of the programme plan: reduce the scope of the decision, for example by asking which part of the envelope the group is ready to recommend, rather than suggest an answer. When a group adopts one of the executive proposals as it stands, the professor asks which question of the challenge list the group would expect from the board and how it would answer, so that the group tests its own reasoning. When the board group is too polite, the professor can play Helena for one question.

The deliverable of the programme plan for Day 3 is observable at the end of the round: each group has a recommendation structured as a narrative, with a chart that fits its question. The professor records on the board, for each group, the governing thought in one line and the question the page did not answer, and photographs the board for Prof. Egon. The pages and charts of the groups stay with the professors of the programme.
